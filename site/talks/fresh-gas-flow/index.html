Four jobs, one knob

  • Response speed. How long the circuit takes to do what you just asked: change FiO2, deepen, wake up.
  • What the patient breathes. At low flow the vaporizer setting and the inspired concentration stop agreeing.
  • Cost. Agent consumption scales almost directly with flow. Roughly 3% of total operating cost is on the table with ideal use.
  • Footprint. Volatiles are potent greenhouse gases, and fresh gas flow is the tap.
Treat fresh gas flow as a setting you revisit at each phase of the case (induction, maintenance, emergence), not one you choose once.

The time constant

The circuit's time constant is its volume divided by the fresh gas flow, and it takes about four of them to approach steady state. Despite the name, it isn't constant: it moves every time you change the flow. That's the chart at the top of the page.

Wash-in and wash-out

Raising the flow speeds up concentration changes, but only up to about minute ventilation. Past that you're mostly buying waste, not speed. Circuit volume and machine design set how much speed you can buy at all.

Low flow doesn't make the circuit less controllable. It makes it slower to answer. Plan changes further ahead, or open the flow for the moment you need speed.
ASK THE LEARNERYou're at 0.5 L/min and the patient moves. Do you turn up the vaporizer, the flow, or push propofol? (Propofol now; if you want the gas to follow, open the flow too. The vaporizer alone takes 20 minutes to matter.)

Maintenance: start here

The dial goes up as the flow comes down

With less fresh gas arriving and ongoing uptake, the inspired concentration sits below the vaporizer setting, so you dial above the number you're targeting. A worked example from a single-lung case, healthy 34-year-old, target 0.7 MAC and FiO2 around 30%: sevoflurane 2.5% at 0.75 L/min held an end-tidal of 1.4–1.5%.

Training wheels: move your alarms first

Before you drop the flow, set the low FiO2 and low end-tidal agent alarms above your true minimum acceptable values. You want to be told you're drifting, not that you've arrived. Early on, check the trends every five to ten minutes.

Induction and emergence

The bookends are where low flow is most demanding, and where the largest single waste happens.

Induction, in order

  1. Cap the flow at minute ventilation. Above MV there's no clinical benefit; 5–6 L/min is plenty for most adults. The exception is a large mask leak.
  2. Prime the circuit properly. High-flow priming against an occluded circuit wastes agent and doesn't work: occlusion closes the valves, so the circuit never fills. Empty the reservoir bag before turning the vaporizer on.
  3. Pause the flow for intubation. High flow with the vaporizer off washes anesthetic into the room. Turn the fresh gas off and leave the vaporizer on instead.
  4. Use overpressure deliberately. Dial above your target. As a rough equivalence: 2% sevoflurane at 8 L/min behaves something like 8% at 2 L/min.
The back half of overpressure. Uptake falls as the case goes on, so inspired concentration climbs toward whatever is still dialed in. The move that got you there quickly will overdose the patient if you forget to come back down.

Emergence

Vaporizer off, 100% oxygen, and keep the flows low. Elimination proceeds through uptake and rebreathing, and with no nitrous there's no added environmental cost to running it out slowly. Open the flow when you want a faster wake-up. Have IV agents drawn up either way.

ASK THE LEARNERWhy does leaving 8 L/min running during intubation, vaporizer off, set you back? (It flushes the agent you just loaded out of the circuit, so you start maintenance from scratch.)

What actually goes wrong

Low flow is safe. It isn't forgiving of inattention, and the failures are specific.

  • Hypoxic mixture. The margin between delivered and inspired oxygen narrows as flow falls, and nitrogen accumulates from the patient. Reliable gas analysis and a low-FiO2 alarm you actually set.
  • Compound A. Minimal risk with modern machines and absorbents, but read the ASA statement on low flows with sevoflurane. The package insert and accepted practice don't say the same thing, and you should be able to explain that gap.
  • "Set equals delivered." Early in a case the patient gets less than you dialed; later, more. Awareness and overdose both live in that gap.
  • The handoff. Handing off at 0.5 L/min to someone who reflexively runs 2 L/min? Say so, including your alarm settings and end-tidal target. Entirely preventable with one sentence.
  • The machine in front of you. Bigger circuits, slower gas cycling, no fresh gas compensation and weaker analysis all push the floor up. On an older workstation, a higher flow is the right answer.
Every number in this talk is a property of the workstation in front of you, not of anesthesia in general.

Flow during TIVA

Propofol wins the volatile argument outright. It doesn't make fresh gas flow stop mattering; it changes what you're spending.

  • Oxygen still has to be produced, and the energy cost scales with how much you run.
  • Absorbent is still consumed, and absorbent use moves opposite to flow.
  • Minute ventilation is still the ceiling worth using.

Zhong and colleagues did the primary work here. The gains are mainly in operating cost and CO2 emissions, and around 4 L/min the environmental benefit is real but marginal. It stops being marginal in insufflation cases, where higher flow with a low oxygen fraction cuts absorbent use.

Takeaways

  • Avoid reflexive high flow. Use minute ventilation as the guide.
  • Heat and humidity are non-inferior with an HME in place, unless either is a specific concern for this patient.
  • Institutional and machine differences dominate. The right number at Denver Health isn't necessarily the right number elsewhere.

Q1 · Basic · ANESHUB equipment & physics

During induction, a patient is breathing isoflurane from a semiclosed circle absorber system. The temperature-compensated vaporizer is set to deliver 1.5%. Which of the following will result in the lowest inspired concentration of isoflurane?

  1. Doubling the length of circle system hoses
  2. Decreasing the barometric pressure
  3. Changing from rubber to plastic breathing hoses
  4. Decreasing fresh gas flow
  5. Exhaustion of the soda lime
Reveal answer

D. During induction, uptake is high. Less fresh gas means less agent arriving and more rebreathed, agent-depleted gas, so the inspired concentration falls further below the dial. Talk link: the three numbers on the first tab.

Q2 · Basic · ANESHUB pharm

For 30 minutes a patient has received oxygen 2 L, nitrous oxide 2 L, and halothane from a vaporizer set at 1%. End-tidal halothane is 0.7%. The most likely explanation for the difference is:

  1. hyperventilation
  2. low cardiac output
  3. the second-gas effect
  4. tissue solubility of halothane
  5. vaporizer temperature less than 19°C
Reveal answer

D. Ongoing uptake into blood and tissue keeps end-tidal below the dial; the more soluble the agent, the bigger the gap. Low cardiac output would do the opposite (end-tidal rises toward inspired faster). Talk link: "the dial is not the dose."

Q3 · Basic · ANESHUB equipment & physics

Compared with a semiclosed circle system, a closed system:

  1. decreases the risk for anesthetic overdose
  2. decreases the risk for rebreathing carbon dioxide
  3. has lower resistance to gas flow
  4. permits calculation of oxygen consumption
  5. permits more rapid changes in anesthetic depth
Reveal answer

D. In a truly closed, leak-free circuit, the oxygen you add to keep volume steady equals what the patient consumes. That's the metabolic floor from the Maintenance tab. E is backwards: closed means the slowest possible time constant. Talk link: Maintenance, "find your floor."

Q4 · Basic · ANESHUB equipment & physics

During general anesthesia through a circle system, the soda lime is exhausted and no fresh absorbent is available. Which is the most appropriate next step to prevent hypercapnia?

  1. Decreasing the dead space of the circle system
  2. Discontinuing nitrous oxide
  3. Increasing the fresh gas flow
  4. Increasing tidal volume
  5. Switching to spontaneous ventilation
Reveal answer

C. Raise fresh gas flow to at least minute ventilation and the circle behaves like an open system: exhaled CO2 goes out the scavenger instead of back to the patient. This is the flip side of low flow: the lower you run, the more you depend on the absorbent. Talk link: The circuit, rebreathing table.

Q5 · Clinical · PassMachine · special techniques

An anesthesiologist starts work at a hospital 1,800 m above sea level. What change in gas flow through the rotameter should he keep in mind?

  1. More than expected
  2. Less than expected
  3. More than expected at high flows, but accurate at low flows
Reveal answer

C. At high flows the flow through the rotameter is turbulent and depends on gas density, which is lower at altitude, so actual flow exceeds the reading. At low flows it's laminar and depends on viscosity, which altitude barely changes, so the reading holds. Denver sits at about 1,600 m, so this one is ours.
