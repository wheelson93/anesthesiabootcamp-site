General Anesthetic Case Flow

A framework for residents rotating through the OR for the first time: what to do, in what order, and where to look, from the time the patient rolls in until wake-up.

The big idea: sweep, don't search

New residents miss things because they jump around: eyes on the monitor, then the IV, then the chart, then back to the vent. The fix is to scan the same physical path every time, so every step has a place where you look for it. The room becomes your checklist.

"Every transition gets a sweep. If you're ever lost, start at the IV pole and walk your eyes across the patient to the machine."

1 · Patient in room → induction

From the doors opening to the attending saying "go ahead."

  1. Before they roll in: log in to the computer + Pyxis, grab the IV bagSpike and prime the bag now. Fumbling with logins while the patient lies there staring at you is how the room gets away from you.
  2. Patient moves over → scan IDScan the band once they're on the table and you can see it.
  3. Connect the IVMake sure the line actually runs before you push anything through it.
  4. Monitors: SpO₂ → BP → ECGThe pulse ox gives you a sat and a heart rate in seconds. Start the BP next so it's cycling. The ECG leads take the longest, so they go last.
  5. Give the premedPer the plan (e.g., midazolam). It goes in after the monitors are on, so you're watching the sat and BP when the sedation hits.
  6. Oxygen mask: strap it, hold it, or ask the RNPick one on purpose. If you're holding it, you can't draw up drugs, so plan for that.
  7. Preoxygenate: goal EtO₂ > 80% on FiO₂ 100%You're washing nitrogen out of the lungs (denitrogenation) so the FRC becomes an oxygen tank. That tank is your apnea time.
  8. Time out → induce
Common miss: pushing induction drugs because "it's been 3 minutes" when the EtO₂ is still 60%. Look at the number, not the clock.

2 · Induction + intubation

From induction to a secured tube with the vent breathing and anesthetic running.

  1. Optimize bed height + intubating conditionsRaise the bed so the patient's face is at about your lower sternum. Line up ear to sternal notch, and ramp if the patient is obese. Fixing your position before induction is free; fixing it once they're apneic costs you seconds.
  2. Induce
  3. Mask ventilate, unless it's an RSIMasking confirms you can ventilate before the paralytic takes over. In an RSI you skip routine masking to limit gastric insufflation (follow your attending's practice on gentle low-pressure breaths).
  4. Wait for the paralytic to circulate Rocuronium at intubating dose: ~3 min · RSI-dose rocuronium: ~45–60 s · Succinylcholine at RSI dose: ~30–60 s. Going in early gets you a patient who bites and bucks.
  5. Intubate → confirmSustained EtCO₂ waveform plus bilateral breath sounds and chest rise. No CO₂ means no tube.

Then the safety sequence

1 Cuff up→2 Vent on→3 Flows down→4 Gas on / TIVA→5 Tape→6 Slow fluids
StepWhy it's in this spot
1 Cuff upNo seal means no reliable ventilation, a leak, and no aspiration protection. Everything else depends on it.
2 Vent onThe patient is apneic. Hand the breathing to the machine so your hands are free.
3 Flows downHigh flows were for preoxygenation. Turning them down saves agent and keeps the gas warm and humid (see the Fresh Gas Flow talk).
4 Gas on / TIVA runningThe induction bolus wears off within minutes, and that's an awareness risk. Watch the ETAA climb. With low flows, the dial may need to start higher than your target.
5 TapeNow secure it. Note the depth (cm at the teeth or lip) and tape the eyes.
6 Slow / stop fluidsThe bag ran wide open to carry the drugs in. Left alone, a liter is gone before incision.
Common miss: taping first while the patient is apneic and the gas is off. Tape can wait 20 seconds; oxygen and anesthetic can't.

3 · Post-intubation → pre-incision

The window while the team positions, preps, and drapes. It feels like downtime, but it isn't.

5 A's
  • Anesthesia on: ETAA climbing, or TIVA running into a line that runs
  • Abx: within 60 min before incision (vanc/FQ within 120)
  • Antithrombotics: SCDs on and pumping ± SQ heparin
  • Antiemetics: risk-stratify, and give dex early
  • Analgesia: the opioid/multimodal plan, and the block if there is one
4 T's
  • Temp monitor: the probe is in and reading
  • Temp modulator: the warmer is on (warm early, because induction drops the core temp)
  • Twitch monitor: placed, with a baseline
  • Tube: OG vs NG, to decompress a stomach full of mask air

Then

Sweep→Time out→Clean up→Chart→Sweep

Sweep before the time out so you can actually answer its questions ("antibiotics given?" "blood available?").

Clean up the workstation
  • Clear the induction mess: wrappers, used syringes, laryngoscope
  • Organize your drugs so emergency drugs are labeled and within reach
  • Get cables and lines off the floor

A clean workstation lets you respond to an emergency fast: you can find the epinephrine and get to the patient without tripping on a cable. It also lets someone else step in. If an attending, a colleague giving you a break, or the next provider takes over, they should be able to read your setup in seconds.

Chart the induction events while they're fresh. Then sweep again, because positioning is when lines get pulled and tubes get kinked.

"Five A's, four T's. Say them out loud until they're automatic."
Common miss: the antibiotic. It's the item the time out checks, and it's the easiest one to forget while the room is busy.

4 · Maintenance

The middle of the case. Quiet is when things drift.

Sweep every 15 minutes

This lines up with when the computer asks for an interval assessment. Let the prompt cue your sweep, not just your charting. The list follows the sweep: IV pole → patient → monitor & machine.

  1. Fluids & dripsIs the IV dripping? Do I want it to be? Are the pumps running at the right rate and still connected?
  2. Trace the tube and every line back to the patientFollow them with your eyes or your hand. Disconnections hide under drapes.
  3. TwitchesCheck the TOF and re-dose the paralytic if the case needs it.
  4. All vitals → optimize what you canBP, HR, SpO₂, EtCO₂, temp, ETAA. Ask "why" about anything that has moved.
  5. Vent settingsAdjust to the EtCO₂, peak/plateau pressures, tidal volume (by IBW), and PEEP.
Common miss: charting the interval assessment without looking up. The prompt is a cue to sweep, not just to click.

5 · Prep: the end of this case and the start of the next

Done during the case, in the quiet stretch before closing, not after drop-off.

End-of-case prep (this patient)
  • Bite block in, before they can bite the tube
  • Zofran given
  • Reversal drawn up, chosen by the twitch count
  • Suction on and within reach
Next case (do it now)
  • Drugs: pull, draw up, and label the next induction
  • Airway: laryngoscope, tube, stylet, oral airway, backup device
  • Goal: when you get back from drop-off, there should be almost nothing left to do
"Turnover time is won during the case before. Every syringe you draw now is one less thing to do with the next patient in the doorway."
Common miss: waiting until the surgeon says "closing" to start any of this. By then you're busy with wake-up.

6 · Closing → wake up

From "we're closing" to a safe extubation.

Closing
  • Gas off + flows down: the agent stays in the circuit and drifts down slowly
  • Re-check the bite block
  • Check tetany, then take the twitch monitor off (tetany hurts, so test it while they're still asleep)
  • Eye tape off, gently
Wake up
  • Flows up, O₂ 100%, to wash the agent out
  • Confirm gas off
  • Propofol ready (laryngospasm, bucking)
  • Mask, syringe, suction in reach (the syringe deflates the cuff)
  • Trash at the head of the bed for the tape and tube

Ready to extubate?

ReversedTOF ratio ≥ 0.9 (quantitative), or sustained tetany
AwakeFollows commands (opens eyes, squeezes a hand, lifts the head)
BreathingRegular spontaneous breaths with adequate tidal volumes
Washed outETAA low
StableHemodynamically stable, oxygenating, warm
Common miss: pulling the tube on a patient who is "moving" but not following commands. Bucking isn't awake. That in-between stage is where laryngospasm happens.
