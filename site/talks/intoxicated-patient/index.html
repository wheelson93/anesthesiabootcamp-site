The Intoxicated Patient in the OR

Two questions for every intoxicated patient: what is this drug doing to them right now, and what will it do to my anesthetic? A positive tox screen answers neither.

2 d
Acute effect Urine screen + Heavy use or EtG

Start at 2 days: the cocaine screen is still positive, but the drug stopped acting long ago. Ask the learner whether they would cancel. Windows are approximate and depend on dose, how often the person uses, and the assay cutoff (Moeller 2017).

Acute and chronic use push in opposite directions

Whether a drug is on board right now, or has been used for months, changes your anesthetic dose. The direction depends on the drug class.

CNS depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzos)↓ MAC, additive sedation and respiratory depression↑ requirements (cross-tolerance)

Four rules for every case

  • Full stomach. Delayed gastric emptying, vomiting, blunted airway reflexes. RSI for emergent cases.
  • Treat the patient, not the urine screen. Metabolites outlast the drug by days to weeks (the chart above). Decide on vitals, ECG, temperature and mental status.
  • Capacity. An intoxicated patient may not be able to consent. Emergencies go ahead on implied consent; elective cases wait.
  • Plan for afterward. Withdrawal, emergence agitation, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, and respiratory depression when intoxication stacks on post-op opioids.
A positive screen tells you the person used the drug at some point in the past few days. It doesn't tell you the drug is still acting.

Alcohol

The most common intoxicant in the trauma bay. It makes the anesthetic easier to give and the patient harder to assess.

Acutely intoxicated

  • MAC goes down, and alcohol adds to the effect of opioids, benzos and induction agents. Wolfson and Freed showed the acute/chronic split in animals with halothane.
  • Hypoglycemia (impaired gluconeogenesis), hypothermia (vasodilation), hypovolemia (diuresis), and aspiration.
  • Alcoholic ketoacidosis can show up in a patient who has been drinking and not eating.
  • Trauma: don't blame a low GCS on alcohol. Image the head.

Chronic heavy use

  • Higher requirements for volatiles, propofol and opioids (cross-tolerance, enzyme induction).
  • Look for cardiomyopathy, cirrhosis and coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, and malnutrition.
  • Give thiamine before glucose to avoid precipitating Wernicke's.

Withdrawal: the post-op problem

  • Tremor and autonomic signs start about 6–24 h after the last drink. Seizures can happen in the first 1–2 days. Delirium tremens typically peaks around 48–96 h.
  • Unexplained post-op tachycardia, hypertension, agitation or fever on day 2–3: think withdrawal. Start a CIWA protocol early.

Cocaine

A local anesthetic that also floods the synapse with catecholamines. It acts for a short time, but its metabolite stays in urine for days.

Mechanism

Blocks reuptake of norepinephrine, dopamine and serotonin, and blocks sodium channels. Expect hypertension, tachycardia, coronary vasospasm, arrhythmias, a wide QRS, hyperthermia and seizures.

The acutely toxic patient

  • Benzodiazepines first. They treat the agitation, hypertension, tachycardia and hyperthermia at the source.
  • Hypertension: vasodilators (nitroglycerin, phentolamine, nicardipine).
  • Wide QRS: sodium bicarbonate.

The beta-blocker debate

  • Lange 1990: intracoronary propranolol made cocaine-induced coronary vasoconstriction worse. That's where "unopposed alpha" comes from, and why the 2008 AHA statement said to avoid beta-blockers.
  • Two 2018 meta-analyses (Pham; Lo) found no increase in MI or death with beta-blockers in cocaine-associated chest pain. Both were observational and low quality.
  • Reasonable approach: not first-line in active toxicity. Labetalol or esmolol after a vasodilator is defensible.

Elective case, positive screen, patient looks fine

If vitals and ECG are normal and QTc is under 500 ms, asymptomatic cocaine-positive patients have not had more intraoperative hemodynamic events (Hill 2006; Moon 2019 and 2021). A 2025 systematic review agrees but calls the evidence thin.

Pearls. Cocaine and succinylcholine are both broken down by plasma cholinesterase, so either can prolong the other. Ketamine adds sympathetic load. Be careful with epinephrine-containing local anesthetic in active toxicity.

Amphetamines and methamphetamine

Longer acting than cocaine, and the chronic user is the one who surprises you on induction.

Acute use

  • MAC goes up. Dextroamphetamine raised halothane MAC by up to about 75% in dogs (Johnston 1972, 1975).
  • Hypertension, tachycardia, hyperthermia, arrhythmias, agitation.

Chronic use

  • Catecholamine stores are depleted. The result is sudden post-induction hypotension that responds poorly to ephedrine, because ephedrine works partly by releasing norepinephrine the nerve terminals no longer have.
  • Use direct agents: phenylephrine, norepinephrine, vasopressin.
  • Look for cardiomyopathy and methamphetamine-associated pulmonary hypertension.

The data

  • Safdari 2022: patients with a positive screen within 48 h had 31% hemodynamic instability vs 6% of controls, even though they got less anesthetic. Waiting up to 7 days lowered the risk but not to baseline.
  • Edwards 2020 (trauma): pressor use tracked how much resuscitation the patient needed more than the tox result.
  • Rizk 2024 (burns): waiting for a negative screen didn't change intraoperative pressor needs.
ASK THE LEARNERMAP 45 after induction, ephedrine 10 mg twice, nothing. Why, and what's next? (Indirect agent, empty stores. Go to phenylephrine or norepinephrine, consider vasopressin.)

Cannabis

Legal here in Colorado and common in our patients. The acute and chronic problems are different.

Acute use (especially smoked)

  • Tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension, and sedation that adds to your drugs.
  • Airway hyperreactivity: laryngospasm and bronchospasm after recent smoking.

Chronic use

  • More propofol needed: Flisberg 2009 found users needed 314 vs 263 mg to allow LMA insertion.
  • Higher post-op pain scores and opioid use. Plan multimodal analgesia.
  • Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome: cyclic vomiting, relieved by hot showers.

ASRA Pain Medicine consensus guidelines (2023)

  • Screen every patient for use.
  • Postpone elective cases if the patient is acutely impaired or lacks decision-making capacity.
  • Warn heavy users that they may need more anesthetic and analgesic.
  • Don't stop cannabis abruptly in patients with cannabis use disorder (withdrawal).

Pull the full guideline for the exact timing thresholds before quoting them.

Opioids

The acute overdose and the chronic user on buprenorphine are two different problems.

Acute overdose

  • Titrate naloxone to breathing, not to full wakefulness. Full reversal triggers acute withdrawal, hypertension and pulmonary edema.
  • Watch for re-narcotization: naloxone lasts 30–90 min, and methadone, extended-release drugs and fentanyl analogs outlast it.
  • Hypoventilation alone can explain both hypercarbia and hypoxemia on room air. That's the backup board question.

Chronic users

  • Expect tolerance and higher requirements. Continue the patient's baseline opioid.
  • Build around multimodal analgesia and regional techniques.
  • Fentanyl is missed by the standard opiate immunoassay and needs its own test.

Board questions

Q1 · Basic · ANESHUB pharm / general

A 25-year-old man needs an exploratory laparotomy after a car crash. He is acutely intoxicated with alcohol. What is the most likely result of the alcohol ingestion?

  1. Hyperdynamic circulation
  2. Hyperglycemia
  3. Hyperthermia
  4. Increased respiratory depression from opioids
  5. Increased sensitivity to neuromuscular blocking drugs
Reveal answer

D. Acute alcohol lowers MAC and adds to the effect of opioids and other sedatives. B and C are backwards: the risks are hypoglycemia and hypothermia. Talk link: the MAC grid and the Alcohol tab.

Q2 · Advanced · ANESHUB general

Patients with acute alcohol intoxication are at increased risk for each of the following complications of general anesthesia EXCEPT:

  1. aspiration of gastric contents
  2. hyperglycemia
  3. hypothermia
  4. ketoacidosis
  5. respiratory depression
Reveal answer

B. Alcohol impairs gluconeogenesis, so the risk is hypoglycemia. The trap is D: alcoholic ketoacidosis is real in a patient who has been drinking and not eating. Talk link: Alcohol tab.

Q3 · Basic · ANESHUB pharm

A 24-year-old woman needs emergency repair of open tibia and fibula fractures. She used cocaine two hours ago. BP is 170/110. Each of the following is useful in managing the hypertension EXCEPT:

  1. hydralazine
  2. labetalol
  3. nitroprusside
  4. phentolamine
  5. propranolol
Reveal answer

E. A pure beta-blocker leaves alpha stimulation unopposed and can worsen coronary vasospasm (Lange 1990). Note the key accepts labetalol, which has some alpha blockade. That's a good lead-in to the 2018 meta-analyses. Talk link: Cocaine tab, the beta-blocker debate.

Q4 · Clinical · PassMachine · cardiovascular

A 25-year-old man comes to the ED with chest pain and palpitations after a party last night. Positive Levine's sign, alert and not agitated. BP 141/91, HR 109, RR 22, venous track marks. ECG shows minimal ST deviation in V1–V6; troponin T <0.03, CK-MB 5.3. Which medication should be the first choice?

  1. Metoprolol
  2. Diazepam
  3. Nitroglycerin
Reveal answer

B. Benzodiazepines come first in cocaine-associated chest pain. They reduce the central sympathetic drive behind the hypertension, tachycardia and vasospasm. Nitroglycerin is next. Talk link: Cocaine tab.

Q5 · Advanced · ANESHUB OB

In pregnant patients, which of the following is an effect of the long-term use of cocaine?

  1. Decreased uterine tone
  2. Decreased thiopental requirement
  3. Increased isoflurane MAC
  4. Increased sensitivity to ephedrine
  5. Lower than expected level of block with tetracaine spinal analgesia
Reveal answer

C per the answer key. This is an older bank item, and it conflicts with the grid on the first tab, where chronic stimulant use lowers MAC through catecholamine depletion. Ask the learner to argue it both ways. A patient with repeated recent use is effectively acutely intoxicated, which raises MAC. Talk link: The one idea.

Backup · Basic · ANESHUB respiratory / critical care

A previously healthy 28-year-old man arrives with a probable opioid overdose. On room air: PaO2 49, PaCO2 76, pH 7.12. Which statement is true?

  1. Aspiration of gastric contents must have occurred
  2. Hypoventilation alone can explain the acidosis and hypoxemia
  3. The hypoxemia is probably due to noncardiogenic pulmonary edema
  4. Naloxone should be administered only if the patient is normothermic
  5. Pure oxygen is contraindicated
Reveal answer

B. Work the alveolar gas equation at sea level: PAO2 ≈ 150 − 76/0.8 ≈ 55, so a PaO2 of 49 means a near-normal A–a gradient. The CO2 explains the hypoxemia. (In Denver, PAO2 is lower still.) Talk link: Opioids tab.
