Intro to the CT Rotation

How the month works, how to set up, and how a bypass case flows from pre-op to the CTICU.

The biggest thing this month is teamwork

CT runs on a relief system. The month goes well or badly based on whether the group runs it honestly and gets the pre-op work done the day before.

The day before

  • All pre-ops done.
  • All inpatient consents done.
  • Nobody leaves until everyone's pre-ops are done.

The relief system

  • Know how it works before day 1.
  • No one jumps the line.
  • CT 7 is the first to leave. CT 1 is the last and is often on a 24-hour shift.

The morning

  • Room set up before you go to pre-op.
  • In pre-op by 7:00, doing your consent and a-line.
Why this matters: nothing is worse than leaving as CT 2 at 8:55, just missing a post-call day, and then still having to consent and do pre-ops. Straight to jail. Finishing the pre-op work as a group is how everyone gets out.

Your first pump case

  • If you haven't done a pump case before, try to see someone set up before you start.
  • Allow ~2+ hours to set up for your first pump case. Come in early.
  • You'll get faster. By graduation it's ~45 minutes if you're rushing.
The only thing you fully control this month is showing up early and getting your stuff done, so that you and your colleagues have a smooth day.

Room setup

Setup is the part you can do on your own, before anyone else arrives. A clean, labeled setup is what keeps a fast case from becoming chaos.

Setup checklist

Adapted from Kenji Tanabe's Cardiac Bypass Cheat Sheet. Your attending's preferences win.

Drugs and syringes
  • Induction plan (often +/- large-dose narcotic/benzo)
  • Phenylephrine bolus syringes
  • Epi bolus syringes: 10 mcg/mL and 100 mcg/mL
  • Norepinephrine push-concentration syringe
  • Vasopressin push-concentration syringe
  • Esmolol bolus
  • Nicardipine bolus + drip
  • Heparin bolus
  • Calcium chloride
  • Antibiotics
  • TXA (or amicar) bolus + drip
Drips
  • Carrier fluid
  • Epi vs dobutamine
  • Norepi / neo vs vasopressin
  • Dexmedetomidine
Lines and monitors
  • Arterial line (placed pre-induction)
  • Cerebral oximetry
  • 2nd IV setup
  • Introducer sheath +/- Swan
  • Ultrasound
  • TEE
  • Epicardial pacer box
Save future you time: build your lab order panels (rewarming labs and post-pump labs) in the first week, so you never have to think about them again.

Start of case → going on bypass

  1. A-line in pre-op. Aim to be there by 7:00.
  2. Patient in the room → click your patient buttons.
  3. Connect their IV and your drip line, and START THE CARRIER.don't skip
    Drips run at a few mL/hr. Without the carrier pushing them, they sit in the tubing and don't reach the patient, and then they arrive all at once when someone flushes the line. In my first week of CT I missed starting the carrier and it caused major problems. Another CA-1 forgot to connect the infusion line the next day, with the same result.
  4. Cerebral oximetry on so you have a baseline. Sometimes a SedLine too.
  5. Standard monitors, with emphasis on getting the a-line transduced first.
  6. Gear up for induction and airway. Low threshold for the videoscope; no need to be a hero. Pre-oxygenate and do your thing.
  7. While you're on the airway, the attending, fellow, or a bored resident without a case will often put a 14g in a wrist or AC.
  8. Airway secure → someone else takes hemodynamics. Push the central line tray to the patient, gown and glove.
    Watch before your first day: How to gown and glove yourself sterilely
  9. Neck prepped, drapes on, central line. Then take off your top gloves, cover the field, and float the Swan.
    Tip: drop a small Tegaderm onto your set-up so you have it to cover the line at the end.
  10. Swan in. Dismantle, clean your tray, and get back to the battle station.
  11. Initial ACT/ABG to perfusion.
  12. Turn on the Swan box.
  13. Antibiotics in. TXA bolus in, TXA drip started.
  14. Organize and label your lines whenever you get a moment.

Sternotomy → cannulation

  1. Sawing → "lungs down." Hold ventilation so the saw doesn't hit lung. Make sure they've had a lot of fentanyl before this; sternotomy is one of the most stimulating moments of the case.
  2. Bring the pressure down to a MAP ~65 for aortic cannulation (some people think of it as SBP 90–100). A lower pressure lowers the risk of aortic dissection when they put the cannula in. Your attending and fellow will guide this early in the month.
  3. Heparin. They'll ask for it. Perfusion confirms the ACT is adequate before bypass.
  4. Aorta cannulated → bring the pressure back up however you need to.
  5. Venous cannula → bypass starts.
"Lungs down" and "drop the pressure" will come fast. Have your phenylephrine and nicardipine syringes where your hands can find them without looking.

Once you're on bypass

The pump is now doing the heart's and lungs' work. Your job changes from driving hemodynamics to switching things off cleanly and watching.

  1. Check urine output and empty it, so your perfusionist can ask you about urine output for the next few hours.
  2. Vent to bypass mode (or off, once at full flow).
  3. Monitors to bypass mode, or silence the alarms.
  4. Pause your Swan box.
  5. Confirm perfusion has started their volatile. Your vaporizer is out of the circuit now, and the patient still needs anesthesia.
Typical bypass targets
MAP ~60–70
Cerebral oximetry (rSO2) within 20% of baseline
Ask your attending what they want for your patient.
Use the time
Label lines, tidy the cart, draw up what you need for coming off, and pull up your lab order panels.

Coming off bypass

This part moves very fast, so I follow a checklist. Your attending and fellow will drive most of it, and your job is to have everything ready before they ask.

On rewarming (~34°C)

Send off platelets and fibrinogen early, so the results are back by the time you need to order products.

Wide Receiver MVP, ABCs

WWarm enough
RRhythm. Do they have a good cardiac rhythm? Pacer box ready.
MMonitors off bypass mode
VVent back on
PPressors / inotropes on and ready
AAntibiotics (post-pump dose)
BBlood and coagulation aids in the room
CCalcium (they'll ask for it)
SSedation. Turn on dexmedetomidine once they're off bypass.

Once truly off

  1. Push antibiotics.
  2. Make sure the blood and platelets you need are in the room.
  3. Give calcium.
  4. Protamine. They'll ask for a large dose and expect it in within a few minutes. Protamine drops the pressure, so you'll alternate pushing protamine and norepinephrine.
  5. Send post-pump labs (use your order set).
Post-pump lab panel
  • CBC, BMP, fibrinogen
  • ABG, iCal
  • PT / PTT / INR
  • +/- TEG with and without heparinase
Where samples go
  • ACT / ABG → perfusion
  • Labs → circulator

Post-pump → CTICU

After protamine, it's resuscitation and then taking down whatever you no longer need.

  1. Resuscitate. Follow your labs/TEG, give products, and keep albumin and products in the room.
  2. Dismantle the extras. Consolidate drips and lines down to what's going to the ICU.
  3. OG tube if not already in.
  4. Sedation for transport. Dexmedetomidine running.
  5. Get ready for CTICU transport. Transport monitor, drips on battery, pressors and emergency drugs with you.
Keep Kenji Tanabe's Cardiac Bypass Cheat Sheet (anesthesiacognitiveaids.com) on you. It has the pressor/vasodilator doses, valve-lesion goals, PA waveforms, and coagulopathy rescue doses in one place.

  • All pre-ops and inpatient consents done the day before. Nobody leaves until everyone's are done.

  2. In room → patient buttons
  3. IV + drip line connected, START THE CARRIER
  4. Cerebral oximetry (baseline) +/- SedLine
  5. Monitors, a-line first
  6. Induction/airway (low threshold for video)
