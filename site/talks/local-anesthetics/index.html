Local anesthetics: selection and dosing

Three physical properties explain almost everything about these drugs. Learn those, learn one formula for max dose, and know what LAST looks like. Additives get their own talk (Part 2: block additives).

One target, three knobs

"Every local anesthetic does the same thing: it plugs the sodium channel from the inside. The differences between them come down to three numbers: pKa, lipid solubility, protein binding."

The three knobs

PropertyControlsWhyExample
pKaOnsetLAs have pKa above 7.4, so most of the drug is ionized at body pH. The closer the pKa is to 7.4, the more neutral (B) form is available to cross the membrane.Lidocaine (7.8) is faster than bupivacaine (8.1).
Lipid solubilityPotency (and toxicity)More lipid-soluble drugs get into the membrane and to the receptor more easily, so you need fewer molecules.Bupivacaine is ~4× as potent as lidocaine: 0.5% bupi ≈ 2% lido.
Protein bindingDurationHighly protein-bound drugs stick to the Na⁺ channel protein longer and leave the site more slowly. (Also binds α1-acid glycoprotein in plasma.)Bupivacaine ~95% bound, lasts much longer than lidocaine ~65%.

pKa: how it actually works

"pKa is the pH at which a drug is half ionized, half not. Local anesthetics are weak bases with pKa above 7.4, so at body pH most of the drug is the charged form, the one that can't get in."

For a weak base, Henderson–Hasselbalch says:

pKa − pH = log ( [BH⁺] / [B] )

  • Every 1 unit the pKa sits above the pH means 10× more ionized than neutral drug.
  • Only the neutral form (B) diffuses through the nerve sheath and membrane. So the fraction that's neutral at tissue pH sets how fast the block comes on.
  • Once inside the axon (slightly more acidic, ~7.1), some B picks up a proton and becomes BH⁺, which is the form that binds the Na⁺ channel. You need both forms: one to get in, one to work.

Where pKa shows up in the OR

  • Infected tissue (pH ~6.5): lidocaine drops from ~28% neutral to ~5%. That's why the I&D still hurts.
  • Bicarbonate nudges pH up toward the pKa, so more drug is neutral: faster onset, less sting. Push it too far with bupivacaine or ropivacaine and the neutral form, which is poorly water-soluble, precipitates.
  • Ion trapping in the fetus: neutral drug crosses the placenta, and an acidotic fetus (pH ~7.1) protonates it and traps it. A distressed fetus accumulates more lidocaine or bupivacaine. Chloroprocaine avoids this because maternal plasma breaks it down within seconds.
  • pKa is not the whole story: concentration matters. 3% chloroprocaine floods the site with so many molecules that even 2.5% neutral is plenty (next box).
The chloroprocaine exceptionChloroprocaine has the highest pKa (~9) but the fastest onset. That's because we give it as a 3% solution: sheer number of molecules (mass action) beats pKa. Good board trap.

What changes onset in real life

  • Acidic tissue (abscess, infection): more drug stays ionized outside the nerve. The dental-abscess block that "won't take."
  • Adding bicarbonate raises pH and speeds onset of lidocaine, mepivacaine and chloroprocaine (1 mL 8.4% per 10 mL lidocaine). Bupivacaine and ropivacaine precipitate if you alkalinize them much.
  • Commercial epinephrine-containing solutions are packaged at pH ~4–5 to keep epi stable, so they have slower onset than plain solution with fresh epi added.
  • Dose and volume beat everything: more molecules at the nerve means faster, denser, longer block.

Differential block: why they lose temperature before motor

Small fibers and myelinated fibers block first, because only a few nodes of Ranvier need to be covered to stop conduction. The usual order:

FiberMyelinJobBlocks
BYes (thin)Preganglionic sympatheticFirst → hypotension, warm foot
CNoSlow pain, temperatureEarly → ice test lost
AδYes (thin)Fast pain, coldEarly
AβYesTouch, pressureLater
AαYes (thick)Motor, proprioceptionLast → why patients can still move after an analgesic epidural

Clinical version: sympathetic block runs ~2 dermatomes above sensory, which runs ~2 above motor in a spinal.

Use-dependent (frequency-dependent) block: LAs bind best to open and inactivated channels, so nerves that are firing a lot get blocked more. That's also why lidocaine works as a class Ib antiarrhythmic in fast, ischemic tissue.

Esters vs amides, then the drugs you'll actually draw up

"If the name has two i's, it's an amide: lidocaine, bupivacaine. One i, it's an ester: chloroprocaine, tetracaine, cocaine."

Esters

Chloroprocaine, tetracaine, cocaine, benzocaine, procaine

  • Broken down by plasma pseudocholinesterase: fast, short t½ (chloroprocaine ~20–40 s in adult plasma).
  • Metabolite is PABA, which is behind the (rare) true LA allergies.
  • Prolonged in pseudocholinesterase deficiency; minimal placental transfer of chloroprocaine because it's broken down in maternal blood.
  • Cocaine is the odd one: liver metabolism, blocks catecholamine reuptake, the only LA that vasoconstricts strongly.
Amides

Lidocaine, mepivacaine, ropivacaine, bupivacaine, prilocaine

  • Metabolized by the liver (CYP1A2, 3A4). Clearance falls with liver disease, heart failure (low hepatic flow), and in neonates.
  • Bound to α1-acid glycoprotein in plasma. Neonates and pregnant patients have less AAG, so more free drug.
  • True allergy is very rare. Most "lidocaine allergy" is epinephrine tachycardia, a vasovagal episode, or the preservative (methylparaben).

Durations are rough single-shot peripheral nerve block ranges; neuraxial and infiltration durations are shorter. They vary with dose, site, and additives. Max doses are the classic package-insert style numbers; see the next tab for why they're starting points, not laws.

Bupivacaine cardiotoxicity: "fast in, slow out"Bupivacaine binds cardiac Na⁺ channels quickly and lets go slowly, so block accumulates beat after beat (worse with tachycardia). It also depresses contractility and mitochondrial energy production. The R-enantiomer is the main offender, which is why ropivacaine (S) and levobupivacaine (S, not sold in the US) were developed. Lidocaine is "fast in, fast out."

Absorption depends on where you inject it

Max dose: the math, and why it's a soft ceiling

"Move the decimal one place right: 1% = 10 mg/mL. That's the whole conversion. Everyone who's ever overdosed a patient on local knew the mg/kg; they forgot the decimal."

Step 1

Concentration → mg/mL

% × 10 = mg/mL
2% lido = 20 mg/mL
0.5% bupi = 5 mg/mL
0.25% bupi = 2.5 mg/mL

Epi 1:200,000 = 5 mcg/mL
1:100,000 = 10 mcg/mL
(1:1,000 = 1 mg/mL; count zeros)

Step 2

Max mg = mg/kg × weight

70 kg, bupi plain:
2.5 × 70 = 175 mg

Use lean or ideal body weight in obesity. Fat doesn't take up much drug or raise your safety margin. Don't exceed the absolute cap even in big people.

Step 3

mg → mL

175 mg ÷ 5 mg/mL = 35 mL
of 0.5% bupivacaine

Then ask whether the block you planned actually fits under that number, before you start injecting.

Mixing two drugs: they add

Toxicity of different local anesthetics is assumed to be additive. Express each one as a fraction of its own max, and keep the sum under 100%.

70 kg. Surgeon wants 20 mL 0.5% bupi in the wound (100 mg = 100/175 = 57%).
Remaining 43% of lidocaine-with-epi max (490 mg) = 210 mg = 10.5 mL of 2% lido w/ epi.

Mixing 1:1 doesn't give you "half the toxicity of each." It gives you a solution with half the concentration of each, and the fractions still add up the same way.

Why the numbers are soft

  • Package-insert max doses were mostly set from small, old studies and don't account for site. 300 mg of lidocaine is very different in an intercostal block than subcutaneously (Rosenberg 2004).
  • Inadvertent intravascular injection can cause toxicity at a tiny fraction of the max: 1–2 mL of bupivacaine into a vertebral artery during an interscalene can seize a patient. Max dose protects you from absorption, not from a needle in a vessel.
  • Tumescent lidocaine (very dilute, with epi, in fat) is given at 35–55 mg/kg because absorption is slow. It's the exception that proves the site rule; it's not a license elsewhere.
  • Airway topicalization for awake intubation: lidocaine up to ~9 mg/kg lean body weight (DAS 2020) because a lot of it is swallowed or never absorbed.

Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)

"Brain first, then heart. Except with bupivacaine, where the heart may not wait for the brain. And except when the patient is asleep, where you'll only see the heart."

Recognize

What it really looks like

  • Only about half of cases look like the textbook. Many skip the prodrome and go straight to seizure or arrhythmia.
  • CV signs: early hypertension and tachycardia, then bradycardia, wide QRS, ventricular arrhythmias, asystole.
  • Timing: most within 5 minutes of injection, but fascial plane blocks and infusions can present up to an hour or more later.
  • Under GA or sedation the CNS phase is hidden. New arrhythmia or hypotension after a block = think LAST.
Treat (ASRA 2020)

Lipid first, then everything else

  1. Stop injecting. Call for help and the LAST kit.
  2. Airway: 100% O₂, ventilate. Hypoxia and acidosis make LAST much worse.
  3. Seizures: benzodiazepine. Small-dose propofol only if hemodynamically stable.
  4. Lipid emulsion 20%, at the first sign of serious toxicity: >70 kg: 100 mL over 2–3 min, then ~200–250 mL over 15–20 min
    ≤70 kg: 1.5 mL/kg bolus, then 0.25 mL/kg/min
    Still unstable: repeat bolus 1–2×, double the infusion
    Upper limit ~12 mL/kg
  5. ACLS with changes: epinephrine ≤1 mcg/kg doses; avoid vasopressin, calcium channel blockers, β-blockers, and more local anesthetic (no lidocaine for the VT). Amiodarone is fine.
  6. Call for CPB/ECMO early if not responding. Prolonged CPR is reasonable.
  7. Monitor ≥4–6 h after a CV event, ≥2 h after a CNS-only event.
Prevention beats lipidLowest effective dose and volume · Ultrasound (cuts LAST risk by roughly 60–65% in registry data) · Aspirate and inject in 3–5 mL increments, waiting a circulation time between · Epinephrine as an intravascular marker (HR ↑ ≥10 or SBP ↑ ≥15 within ~1 min of 10–15 mcg) · Standard monitors and someone watching the patient, not just the screen.

Choosing one: three questions

"Ask: How fast do I need it? How long should it last? Do they need to move afterward? Then check the dose budget."

Rebound painA great long-acting block can produce a miserable 2 AM when it wears off, especially after bony surgery. Tell the patient when it'll wear off, and have them start oral analgesics before it does. This is one reason additives and catheters exist (Part 2).

Drawing it in 5 minutes

  1. The membrane. Two lines for the axon membrane. BH⁺ ⇌ B outside, B crosses, BH⁺ blocks the Na⁺ channel inside. Say: "neutral to get in, charged to work."
  2. Three knobs. Write pKa → onset, lipid → potency, protein binding → duration. Put lido and bupi under each.
  3. The decimal. Write "1% = 10 mg/mL" big. Do one problem with the learner's own weight.
  4. The site ladder. IV → intercostal → caudal → epidural → brachial → sciatic → subQ. Arrow down labeled "less absorption."
  5. The ladder of toxicity. Staircase going up: tinnitus, seizure, coma, heart. Then cross it out for bupi and write "heart can come first."
  6. Lipid. "100 mL, then 250 over 20." That's the number to remember for a 70+ kg adult.

Questions that make it stick

  • Why does an abscess drain hurt even after you've infiltrated half a syringe of lidocaine?
  • You have 0.5% bupi and 2% lido with epi for an 85 kg (IBW 70 kg) patient getting a TAP block plus surgeon infiltration. Run the budget out loud.
  • Why is 3% chloroprocaine the fastest epidural top-up when its pKa is the worst of the bunch?
  • Patient under GA, five minutes after an interscalene: wide-complex tachycardia. What do you grab first?
  • Why can an epidural run of 0.1% ropivacaine leave a laboring patient walking-numb but not weak?

Board-style questions

Written for this talk in ABA/ITE style.

1. Which property of a local anesthetic best predicts its duration of action?
A. pKaB. Lipid solubilityC. Protein bindingD. Molecular weight

C. Protein binding tracks duration (bupivacaine ~95%). pKa tracks onset; lipid solubility tracks potency.

2. A 60 kg patient will receive 0.5% bupivacaine (no epi) for a block. Surgeon then wants to infiltrate with 1% lidocaine (no epi). After 15 mL of bupivacaine, what's the most lidocaine that keeps total dose within classic limits?
A. 7 mLB. 13 mLC. 20 mLD. 27 mL

B. Bupi max = 2.5 × 60 = 150 mg; 15 mL × 5 mg/mL = 75 mg = 50%. Lido max = 4.5 × 60 = 270 mg; 50% = 135 mg = 13.5 mL of 1%.

3. The same dose of lidocaine will produce the highest peak plasma concentration after which block?
A. EpiduralB. IntercostalC. FemoralD. Interscalene

B. Intercostal > caudal > epidural > brachial plexus > sciatic/femoral > subcutaneous.

4. An 80 kg patient becomes hypotensive with a wide QRS 3 minutes after a 30 mL interscalene block with 0.5% bupivacaine. Besides airway and calling for help, the most appropriate next step is:
A. Lidocaine 100 mg IVB. Vasopressin 40 units IVC. 20% lipid emulsion 100 mL IV over 2–3 minD. Epinephrine 1 mg IV

C. Lipid early. Avoid vasopressin and more local anesthetic; if epinephrine is needed, keep doses ≤1 mcg/kg.

5. During awake fiberoptic intubation with benzocaine spray, SpO₂ falls to 86% and doesn't improve with 100% O₂. ABG PaO₂ is 380 mmHg. Best treatment:
A. Methylene blue 1–2 mg/kg IVB. Hydroxocobalamin 5 g IVC. Lipid emulsion 1.5 mL/kgD. Sodium nitrite

A. Methemoglobinemia: the pulse ox reads near 85% regardless of true saturation (saturation gap). Confirm with co-oximetry. Avoid methylene blue in G6PD deficiency.

6. Bupivacaine is more cardiotoxic than lidocaine mainly because it:
A. Has a lower pKaB. Dissociates slowly from cardiac Na⁺ channelsC. Is metabolized by pseudocholinesteraseD. Causes coronary vasoconstriction

B. "Fast in, slow out" leads to cumulative block, worsened by tachycardia. The R-enantiomer is the main culprit.

7. Lidocaine (pKa 7.8) is injected into inflamed tissue with a pH of 6.8. What is the approximate ratio of ionized to non-ionized drug?
A. 1:10B. 1:1C. 10:1D. 100:1

C. For a weak base, pKa − pH = log([BH⁺]/[B]) = 7.8 − 6.8 = 1, so 10:1. Only ~9% can cross the membrane, so onset is slow and the block may fail.
