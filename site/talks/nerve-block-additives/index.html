Block additives: what actually makes a block last longer

Epinephrine, dexamethasone, clonidine, dexmedetomidine, buprenorphine, and the rest. How much each one buys you, what it costs, and whether it needs to be in the syringe or just in the IV. Part 2 of the local anesthetic series (Part 1: local anesthetics).

Printable one-page handout →

Why add anything?

"A single shot of bupivacaine gives you most of a night. Surgery pain lasts 2–3 days. Additives are an attempt to stretch one injection across that gap, without a catheter."

Four questions for any additive

  1. How much does it add? Hours of analgesia, not just "statistically longer."
  2. Does it need to be perineural? If IV gets you most of the benefit, IV avoids the off-label perineural question.
  3. What does it cost the patient? Hypotension, bradycardia, sedation, PONV, hyperglycemia.
  4. Is it safe on the nerve? Preservative-free, compatible with the LA, no neurotoxicity signal.
Off-label, and say soOnly epinephrine is a labeled additive for peripheral nerve blocks in the US. Perineural dexamethasone, dexmedetomidine, clonidine and buprenorphine are all off-label. They're widely used and reasonably well studied, but it's worth knowing that when you're consenting a patient or when a nurse asks why there's dex in your block syringe.
Longer isn't always betterA 30-hour block after an ankle fracture is great. A 30-hour dense block after a shoulder scope in someone who lives alone, or a long femoral block in someone who needs to walk to the bathroom, is a fall risk. Match the duration to the surgery and the patient's day.

Epinephrine: the original additive

"Epi does three jobs: it keeps the drug at the nerve, it lowers the peak blood level, and it tells you when you're in a vessel."

What it does
  • Vasoconstriction (α1) → slower uptake → longer block and a lower peak plasma level (roughly 20–30% lower for lidocaine).
  • Prolongs lidocaine and mepivacaine a lot (often 50–100%). Adds little to bupivacaine and ropivacaine, which are already long and highly protein-bound (ropivacaine also vasoconstricts on its own).
  • Mild α2-mediated analgesia, mainly relevant in neuraxial use.
  • Intravascular marker: 10–15 mcg IV produces a quick rise in HR and BP.
Numbers

1:200,000 = 5 mcg/mL (standard)
1:400,000 = 2.5 mcg/mL (enough as marker)
Test dose: 3 mL of 1:200,000 = 15 mcg

Positive test: HR ↑ ≥10 bpm, or SBP ↑ ≥15 mmHg, within about 1 minute. In children under GA, T-wave amplitude ↑ ≥25% is the most sensitive sign.

β-blocked patients: the HR rise may not happen. Watch the blood pressure instead.

Where to be careful

  • End-artery myth: lidocaine with epinephrine in fingers, toes, nose and penis has been studied extensively and appears safe in patients with normal circulation (the WALANT literature). Many surgeons now prefer it. Phentolamine reverses it if needed.
  • Nerve blood flow: epi lowers perineural blood flow. In nerves that are already compromised (severe diabetic neuropathy, prior chemotherapy), many people leave it out or use 1:400,000.
  • Cardiac disease: tachycardia, ischemia, arrhythmias in patients with severe CAD, AS, or tachyarrhythmias. A few mL of 1:200,000 is usually fine; 40 mL of it is 200 mcg.
  • Commercial premixed solutions are acidic (pH ~4–5, with sodium metabisulfite) to keep epi stable, so onset is slower than adding fresh epi to plain solution.
  • Neuraxial: epi 0.1–0.2 mg in a spinal prolongs tetracaine and lidocaine more than bupivacaine, and can delay voiding.

Dexamethasone: the most-used, most-argued additive

"Dex prolongs a long-acting block by most of a day. The argument is about where to put it. The answer: put it in the IV and you'll get most of it."

The benefit
  • With bupivacaine or ropivacaine, dex prolongs analgesia by roughly 6–8 hours over LA alone. Also lowers pain scores, opioid use, and PONV, and seems to blunt rebound pain.
  • Perineural vs IV: perineural wins, but only by about 2–3 hours in 2024 meta-analyses (≈2 h for interscalene, ≈2.7 h for lower-extremity blocks). The authors called it unlikely to be clinically relevant.
  • That suggests much of the effect is systemic (anti-inflammatory), not something happening only at the nerve.
  • Ceiling dose: perineural 2–4 mg seems to do about as much as 8 mg.
Dosing

Perineural: 2–4 mg (preservative-free)
IV: 0.1–0.2 mg/kg (commonly 4–10 mg)

Mechanism isn't settled. Proposed: glucocorticoid receptor effects on C-fiber K⁺ channels, local vasoconstriction, and systemic anti-inflammatory action.

If you're already giving 4–8 mg IV for PONV, you're already getting most of the block benefit. Don't give a second dose in the block just by habit.

The costs

  • Hyperglycemia: a single 4–8 mg dose raises glucose modestly for ~24 h; more in diabetics. Usually not dangerous, but worth a check in brittle diabetics.
  • Infection: the PADDI trial (NEJM 2021, ~8,700 patients) found 8 mg IV dex non-inferior to placebo for surgical site infection, including in diabetics.
  • Compatibility: dex sodium phosphate is alkaline. Mixtures with ropivacaine have formed crystals in lab studies. Draw up fresh, use preservative-free dex, look at the syringe.
  • Perineural use is off-label. Animal neurotoxicity data are reassuring, but the 2024 meta-analyses still recommended IV given the small difference.
  • Perineal burning or itching with fast IV push. Push slowly in awake patients.
What to say at the bedside"I'm giving dexamethasone through the IV. It'll help with nausea and should make your block last several hours longer."

α2 agonists: clonidine and dexmedetomidine

"Same idea, different strength. Dexmedetomidine is the more selective, more potent cousin of clonidine, so it adds more time and more side effects."

Clonidine

α2:α1 selectivity ≈ 200:1
Perineural 0.5–1 mcg/kg (≤150 mcg)
Adds ~1.5–2 h

  • Works best with intermediate-acting LAs (mepivacaine, lidocaine); smaller effect with bupivacaine/ropivacaine (Pöpping 2009).
  • Dose-dependent hypotension, bradycardia, sedation.
  • Neuraxial: intrathecal 15–75 mcg, caudal 1–2 mcg/kg in children (also lowers emergence agitation). Epidural clonidine's label carries a boxed warning against obstetric, postpartum, and perioperative use because of hemodynamic instability.
Dexmedetomidine

α2:α1 selectivity ≈ 1600:1
Perineural 0.5–1 mcg/kg (often 30–100 mcg)
Adds ~4–5 h

  • Schnabel 2018 meta-analysis: ~4.9 h added analgesia with perineural dexmed. Block onset is often faster too.
  • Bradycardia and hypotension risk ~3× higher, plus sedation. Lower doses (30–50 mcg) seem to keep most of the benefit with fewer side effects.
  • Perineural vs IV dexmed: the data are mixed. Schnabel found no significant difference; other meta-analyses favor perineural. Off-label either way.
  • Animal data suggest it may reduce perineural inflammation rather than harm the nerve.
Who shouldn't get themBradycardic, heart block, hypovolemic, already on α2 agonists or high-dose β-blockers, elderly ambulatory patients who need to go home alert. The extra sedation is a feature in the OR and a problem in phase II recovery.

Everything else, and the non-drug ways to make a block last

Buprenorphine

Perineural 150–300 mcg
Adds roughly 6–8 h (wide range)

Partial μ agonist that also blocks Na⁺ channels like a local anesthetic. Real prolongation, but PONV goes up several-fold unless you give prophylaxis. Off-label.

Sodium bicarbonate

1 mL 8.4% per 10 mL lidocaine/mepivacaine
0.3 mL per 10 mL chloroprocaine

Speeds onset and takes the sting out of infiltration. Doesn't prolong. Don't add to bupivacaine or ropivacaine beyond a tiny amount: they precipitate.

Opioids on a peripheral nerve

Fentanyl, morphine, sufentanil perineurally: no convincing benefit over the same dose IV. Opioids work great neuraxially (spinal/epidural) because that's where the receptors are.

Not recommended

Magnesium, midazolam, ketamine, tramadol, neostigmine perineurally: small or inconsistent benefit, side effects, and neurotoxicity concerns for some (midazolam, ketamine preservatives). Skip them.

A reasonable defaultLong-acting LA (± epi as a marker) + dexamethasone 4–8 mg IV for most patients. Add perineural dex or low-dose dexmed when you need every hour and the patient can tolerate the side effects. Use a catheter when you need more than ~24 h.

Drawing it in 5 minutes

  1. The gap. Timeline: block lasts ~12 h, pain lasts ~48 h. Shade the gap. "Everything today is about that gap."
  2. The bars. Horizontal bars for added hours: epi tiny, clonidine 2, dexmed 4–5, dex 6–8. Draw dex twice (IV and perineural) a couple hours apart.
  3. The vessel. Nerve with a capillary next to it. Squeeze the capillary with epi: drug stays put, less gets into the blood, and a test dose makes the HR jump.
  4. The α2 trick. A nerve that can't reset (Iₕ blocked) stays quiet longer.
  5. The price tags. Under each bar, write the cost: HR, BP, sedation, sugar, nausea.

Questions that make it stick

  • You gave 8 mg dex IV for PONV at induction. Should you also put 4 mg in the interscalene? What would it add?
  • Why does epi make a big difference to a lidocaine block and a small one to a bupivacaine block?
  • Your patient is on metoprolol. How do you read your epi test dose?
  • Ambulatory 78-year-old, foot surgery, lives alone. Do you add dexmedetomidine to the popliteal?
  • When is a catheter better than any additive?

Board-style questions

Written for this talk in ABA/ITE style.

1. How many micrograms of epinephrine are in 20 mL of lidocaine 1:200,000?
A. 10 mcgB. 50 mcgC. 100 mcgD. 200 mcg

C. 1:200,000 = 1 g/200,000 mL = 5 mcg/mL × 20 mL = 100 mcg.

2. Epinephrine added to a local anesthetic will most prolong the block produced by:
A. BupivacaineB. RopivacaineC. LidocaineD. Liposomal bupivacaine

C. Short- and intermediate-acting drugs that vasodilate gain the most. Bupivacaine and ropivacaine are already long-acting and highly protein-bound; ropivacaine vasoconstricts on its own.

3. A patient on a β-blocker receives a 3 mL test dose of lidocaine 1.5% with epinephrine 1:200,000 through an epidural catheter. Which finding most reliably indicates intravascular placement?
A. HR ↑ by 5 bpmB. SBP ↑ by ≥15 mmHgC. Tinnitus at 5 minutesD. Bilateral leg weakness

B. β-blockade blunts the heart-rate response, so the BP rise is the more reliable sign. Leg weakness suggests intrathecal placement.

4. Compared with IV dexamethasone, perineural dexamethasone added to an interscalene block:
A. Doubles the duration of analgesiaB. Prolongs analgesia by about 2 hoursC. Has no effect on durationD. Is FDA-approved for this use

B. 2024 BJA meta-analysis: about 2 h longer, low-quality evidence, of questionable clinical relevance. Perineural use is off-label.

5. The most likely adverse effect of adding 100 mcg dexmedetomidine to a supraclavicular block is:
A. HyperglycemiaB. BradycardiaC. MethemoglobinemiaD. Pruritus

B. Bradycardia, hypotension and sedation are dose-dependent with perineural dexmedetomidine.

6. Adding sodium bicarbonate to which local anesthetic is most likely to cause precipitation?
A. LidocaineB. MepivacaineC. ChloroprocaineD. Bupivacaine

D. Bupivacaine and ropivacaine precipitate when alkalinized beyond a very small amount.
