PONV: two agents for everyone

The 5th consensus guidelines stopped asking who deserves prophylaxis. Two agents is the floor for every adult. Your judgment now goes into what you add on top and what baseline risk you remove.

Start with the 34-year-old nonsmoking woman going home after a lap chole, then switch to a 62-year-old male smoker having a shoulder scope under a block (clear every chip). Ask what each one gets. The second answer is the hinge of the talk.

Prophylaxis is no longer risk-gated

Guideline 3 now reads "administer PONV prophylaxis using 2 interventions in adults at any risk." The 2020 guidelines reserved two agents for moderate and high risk. Almost everything else in the update is refinement around that pivot.

Draw this: the staircase and the floor

  1. The old staircase. Left half of the board: four steps, Apfel factors along the bottom (0, 1–2, 3, 4), agents up the side (0, 2, 3, 4). "This is what you were taught."
  2. The floor. A thick line straight across at "2 agents," and cross out everything below it. "Nobody gets less than two."
  3. Two columns. Right half: REMOVE RISK (TIVA, regional, opioid-sparing analgesia, euvolemia, no nitrous) beside ADD DRUGS (5-HT3, dexamethasone, D2 antagonist, NK-1, olanzapine). "Your judgment lives here now, and the left column is free."
  4. The rescue arrow. A box labeled "gave ondansetron," an arrow curving back into itself with a fat X through it, and a second arrow sideways to "different class." "This is the most common PACU error in the building."

Why they did it

  • Risk stratification fails in practice. Adherence is poor, and the misses land on high-risk patients, the ones the protocol was built for.
  • The thresholds were never real. How much opioid counts as "postop opioids"? How many cigarettes make a smoker? Prior PONV is confounded by whatever they got last time.
  • The drugs are safe at prophylactic doses. When the cost of treating shrinks to near zero, you stop rationing.
  • Equity. Registry data show lower-SES and Black patients get fewer antiemetics after adjustment. "Everyone gets two" removes the discretion where disparity enters.
Ondansetron 4 mg plus dexamethasone 4–8 mg is a defensible floor for essentially every general anesthetic. Stop asking whether the patient has earned prophylaxis; ask what to add.

Risk still matters, differently

The score no longer decides whether to treat. It decides how far past two you go, and it flags the patients in whom vomiting is a medical problem: wired jaws, raised ICP, fresh tympanoplasty, free flap.

A review of 8 prediction models (62 publications, 81,834 patients) ranked the simplified Apfel score first. Apfel factors: female, nonsmoker, prior PONV or motion sickness, postop opioids. With 0/1/2/3/4 factors, incidence is about 10/20/40/60/80%. Koivuranta adds surgery over 60 minutes.

Bolting extra factors onto the models doesn't improve prediction, even factors with odds ratios of 2–3. That's why "but my patient also has X" rarely changes the math.

PDNV is scored separately

Female, age < 50, prior PONV, opioids in PACU, nausea in PACU. With 0–5 factors, incidence is about 7/20/28/53/60/89%. Overall, 37% of US outpatients have PDNV in the first 48 hours.

Combinations

Combinations that beat at least one of their components: NK-1 + 5-HT3, NK-1 + dexamethasone, 5-HT3 + dexamethasone. They aren't interchangeable: aprepitant + palonosetron outperforms dexamethasone + metoclopramide. "Pick a different class" is the floor, not the strategy.

Free interventions

TIVA (roughly equal to volatile plus one antiemetic, A1), regional, opioid-sparing analgesia, euvolemia, avoiding hypotension and nitrous. PC6 acupoint stimulation has a Cochrane review behind it (A1) and adds to drugs. Sugammadex lowers PONV versus neostigmine, but not in patients on propofol TIVA.

ASK THE LEARNERWhy dexamethasone at induction and ondansetron at the end? (Dex takes hours to work through gene transcription; ondansetron's 3–6 h half-life should cover emergence and PACU.)

Safety updates that end old arguments

These are the objections you'll hear from surgeons, endocrinology and cautious colleagues.

They believeActually
"Dex raises infection risk"PADDI (n = 8,725): a single 8 mg dose was non-inferior to placebo for 30-day SSI, in every subgroup including diabetes.
"No dex for diabetics"Transient glucose rise of about 36 mg/dL (2 mmol/L), no effect on wound healing.
"Ondansetron causes torsades"32,737 patients: no TdP deaths attributable to 4 mg, including 4,331 with QTc > 450 ms.
"Droperidol is dangerous"At 0.625–1.25 mg, QT effect ≈ ondansetron's, and combining them doesn't stack. No VT signal at 0.625 mg.
"Reglan is a fine second agent"Not recommended for prophylaxis; inferior to dex, droperidol and ondansetron.
The one new "don't." Transdermal scopolamine is linked to postoperative urinary retention, especially after urogynecologic surgery (A3). Reach for a different agent there.
ASK THE LEARNERYour patient is diabetic. Are you giving dexamethasone? (Fishing for hesitation, then PADDI and the 36 mg/dL number.)

Rescue and post-discharge

The core rule is unchanged: treat breakthrough PONV with a class the patient hasn't already received. What's new is the specificity.

After failed ondansetron

  • Redosing ondansetron, or switching to another 5-HT3, adds nothing.
  • Promethazine 6.25 mg beats redosing ondansetron. Six point two five, not 12.5 or 25.
  • Droperidol, or amisulpride 10 mg, are other new-class options. Propofol 20–40 mg works when you need something now.
  • More than 6 hours out and nothing else available? A second 5-HT3 or butyrophenone dose is reasonable.

Don't redose in PACU

  • Palonosetron (40-h half-life)
  • Aprepitant / fosaprepitant
  • Scopolamine patch (2–4 h onset)
  • Dexamethasone as rescue

Post-discharge

37% of outpatients have PDNV in 48 hours, with no IV and no nurse. High-risk patients should leave with a long-acting antiemetic on board (B1): olanzapine 10 mg PO with ondansetron and dex (about 60% less 24-h PDNV), or palonosetron.

Pediatrics

In children the outcome is POV, not PONV, because nausea can't be reliably assessed. Apfel discriminates poorly in kids.

Risk factorsProphylaxis
0One agent: ondansetron or dexamethasone
1–2Two agents: ondansetron + dexamethasone
3+At least two; don't reflexively add a third. Work on baseline risk instead.

Zero-factor children still get an agent because POV rates at zero and one factor are nearly identical. The panel endorses an ad hoc additive count drawing on the VPOP and POVOC scores plus post-pubertal female, anticholinesterase use and volatile vs TIVA.

  • Ondansetron 0.05–0.1 mg/kg. Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg, max 4 mg.
  • TIVA beats volatile (A1). If full TIVA isn't feasible, a subhypnotic propofol infusion may help.
  • Intranasal dexmedetomidine premed beats midazolam or clonidine. IV dex 0.25–1 mcg/kg also reduces the oculocardiac reflex in strabismus surgery.

Board questions

Five questions from the anes qvault question bank, in the order the talk covers them. Ask the question, let them commit to an answer, then reveal.

Q1 · Clinical · PassMachine · pharmacology

A 50-year-old woman has severe nausea and vomiting after surgery for ulcerative colitis. She received TIVA, aprepitant and ondansetron. You give droperidol since nothing else has worked. Which is a potential concern with droperidol?

  1. Ventricular escape
  2. Long QT syndrome
  3. Bradycardia
Reveal answer

B. QT prolongation is behind the 2001 black box. The talk's nuance: at 0.625–1.25 mg the QT effect is about the same as ondansetron's, and combining them doesn't stack. Also note what she already got: three classes, so droperidol is the right "new class" move. Talk link: Safety, and Rescue.

Q2 · Clinical · PassMachine · pharmacology

A 45-year-old asks in preop clinic about scopolamine to reduce PONV. Which is a possible side effect?

  1. Blurred vision
  2. Muscle weakness
  3. QT interval prolongation
  4. Pruritus
Reveal answer

A. Anticholinergic effects: blurred vision (especially after touching the patch, then the eye), dry mouth, sedation, confusion in the elderly, and the new one in this edition, urinary retention after urogynecologic surgery. Talk link: Safety, "the one new don't."

Q3 · Basic · ANESHUB pharm

Severe nausea and vomiting in the PACU is most effectively treated with an antagonist at which receptor?

  1. Alpha-adrenergic
  2. Beta-adrenergic
  3. Dopamine
  4. GABA
  5. Glutamate
Reveal answer

C, from the options given (5-HT3 isn't on the list; this is an older item). It fits the current rescue rule anyway: after 5-HT3 prophylaxis, a D2 antagonist (droperidol, amisulpride 10 mg, haloperidol) is the classic new class. Talk link: Rescue.

Q4 · Basic · ANESHUB pharm

A 19-year-old woman has severe nausea and vomiting after laparoscopy. After prochlorperazine 10 mg IM, she develops spasms of the face, neck and tongue. The most appropriate management is:

  1. Baclofen
  2. Diphenhydramine
  3. Epinephrine
  4. Hydrocortisone
  5. Naloxone
Reveal answer

B. Acute dystonia from D2 blockade; an anticholinergic antihistamine (or benztropine) reverses it. The same risk comes with metoclopramide, one more reason it fell off the prophylaxis list. Talk link: The agents.

Q5 · Basic · ANESHUB general

100 children having strabismus surgery are randomized: 50 get a new antiemetic, 50 get placebo. 15 in the treatment group and 37 in the control group vomit. Which analysis is most appropriate for significance?

  1. Analysis of variance
  2. Chi-square analysis
  3. Linear regression and correlation coefficient
  4. Student's t-test
  5. Wilcoxon rank sum test
Reveal answer

B. Two groups, a yes/no outcome: a 2 × 2 table, so chi-square. Bonus: absolute risk reduction is 74% − 30% = 44%, so the number needed to treat is about 2.3. Good moment to talk about grades: A1 means multiple RCTs with meta-analysis; A3 is a single trial, and several of the new drug recommendations sit there. Talk link: Pediatrics, and the grading key below.

Source: Gan TJ et al., Fifth Consensus Guidelines for the Management of Postoperative Nausea and Vomiting, Full Report (Appendix 1), American Society for Enhanced Recovery and Perioperative Medicine, 2025; literature through September 2024.

Grading key: A = RCT evidence (A1 multiple RCTs with meta-analysis; A2 multiple RCTs; A3 single RCT). B = observational (B1 with statistics; B2 without). C = equivocal (C1 meta-analysis found no difference; C2 qualitative). Say the grade out loud for anything you're asking someone to change their practice over.
