The perfect SID storm

The liver clears roughly half to two-thirds of systemic lactate and nearly all the citrate in blood products. During and after hepatectomy, both jobs fall to a clamped or shrunken organ.

Draw this: the double impairment

Repair systemNormallyDuring / after hepatectomy
Lactate clearanceLiver clears most of itStops during Pringle; reduced by remnant size afterward
Citrate metabolismCleared within minutesSlowed, so hypocalcemia lasts longer than in other cases
GlucoseLiver holds euglycemiaRises with ischemia; can fall late if the remnant is poor

In this case, trajectory beats any single number. A lactate of 5 that's falling is expected; a lactate of 4 that isn't is a problem.

Low-CVP dissection and the Pringle

Deliberate hypovolemia during parenchymal transection, then a clamped liver quietly building a lactate debt.

Low-CVP phase

  • Target CVP ≤ 5 (ideally ≤ 3) to limit hepatic venous backbleeding: restricted fluids, with or without Trendelenburg.
  • Hold MAP with norepinephrine, not boluses. A saline bolus for every MAP dip narrows SID and makes the CVP target harder to hold. If volume is needed, 5% albumin restores Atot.
  • Vasopressin lowers portal inflow; some surgeons like that during transection and some don't. Ask yours.

Pringle: the silent debt

Portal triad clamped. The liver is ischemic, and lactate accumulates in the liver, not yet in the blood. The gas during clamping can look deceptively stable.

Intermittent Pringle. About 15 min clamped, 5 min released, reduces the ischemic debt. Each release sends a smaller lactate bolus, so expect a sawtooth on serial gases.

Unclamping and reperfusion

The biggest acid-base event of the case. The stored hepatic lactate floods the circulation, and a liver that may be 20–30% of its original size has to clear it.

A lactate of 5–7 just after unclamping, in a patient who was stable, is hepatic washout, not shock. Protect MAP and hepatic perfusion; don't chase the number with pressors or crystalloid.
  • Hold fluids briefly and let the CVP come back up; the bleeding risk has fallen.
  • CaCl2 1 g before release if iCa < 1.1.
  • Gas every ~15 min. If lactate is still rising 30 minutes after release, suspect ongoing ischemia.
  • No bicarb for the spike. Give the remnant time and perfusion pressure.
ASK THE LEARNERLactate 5.8 ten minutes after release, MAP 70 on a little norepinephrine. What do you do? (Very little: support MAP, give calcium, recheck in 15 minutes and look at the direction.)

Small-for-size syndrome

The feared complication when the future liver remnant is too small: roughly under 20–25% of total liver volume in a normal liver, under about 40% in cirrhosis. Non-clearing lactate, falling glucose, persistent hypocalcemia and worsening coagulopathy together are the signature. A rising lactate at closure should reach the ICU as a named concern, not as "metabolic acidosis."

The handoff

Separate the components, give the trajectory, and name the red flags.

"pH 7.26 at closure with a lactate of 4.8. This is expected post-hepatectomy physiology, not hemorrhagic shock: lactate peaked at 6.1 after a 42-minute Pringle and has been falling. His apparent SID is about 19: a chloride component (Cl 111) on top of the residual lactate. Albumin is 1.8, which is hiding about 6 mEq/L more. iCa was 0.88 after reperfusion; he got 3 g of CaCl2 and the last iCa was 1.06, so check every 4 hours because his citrate clearance will be slow. If lactate isn't falling by 2 hours, or glucose starts dropping, that's a small-for-size concern and needs hepatology."

Board questions

Five questions from the anes qvault question bank, in the order the talk covers them. Ask the question, let them commit to an answer, then reveal.

Q1 · Clinical · TrueLearn · GI / hepatic

Which set of hemodynamic values is most typical of end-stage liver disease? (Cardiac output L/min, SVR dyn·s·cm⁻⁵, hepatic venous pressure gradient mmHg.)

  1. CO 4, SVR 1,200, HVPG > 10
  2. CO 8, SVR 700, HVPG > 10
  3. CO 4, SVR 1,200, HVPG < 5
  4. CO 8, SVR 700, HVPG < 5
Reveal answer

B. Hyperdynamic circulation (high output, low SVR from splanchnic vasodilation) plus portal hypertension (HVPG > 10 is clinically significant). This is the baseline you're starting from before any low-CVP strategy. Talk link: Before incision.

Q2 · Basic · ANESHUB general

A 57-year-old with hepatocellular disease is scheduled for colectomy. Albumin is 2.4 g/dL; PT is 16 s (control 12). Which is true about anesthetic management?

  1. Atracurium will have a prolonged duration of action
  2. The risk of perioperative hyperglycemia is increased
  3. Mivacurium will have a shortened duration of action
  4. The unbound fraction of thiopental will be increased
  5. Vitamin K will normalize the prothrombin time
Reveal answer

D. Less albumin means more free drug for highly protein-bound agents. Same low albumin, second consequence: Atot is down, so the pH is flattering you. E is a trap: in synthetic failure, vitamin K won't fix the PT. Talk link: Before incision.

Q3 · Basic · ANESHUB general

Which is most likely to increase hepatic blood flow during general anesthesia?

  1. Adding PEEP
  2. Switching from isoflurane to enflurane
  3. Increased PaCO2
  4. Moderate controlled hypotension
  5. Intrathecal morphine
Reveal answer

C. Hypercarbia increases hepatic (mostly portal) blood flow; hypocarbia, PEEP and hypotension reduce it. Relevant twice here: PEEP raises hepatic venous pressure during low-CVP transection, and a hypotensive remnant clears lactate worse. Talk link: Low-CVP & Pringle.

Q4 · Clinical · PassMachine · hematologic

During liver transplantation the IVC is injured and the patient receives 12 units of PRBCs. In the anhepatic phase, with minimal bleeding, he gradually becomes hypotensive with a narrow pulse pressure, and the QT is prolonged. The most likely mechanism?

  1. Hyperkalemia
  2. Bacterial sepsis
  3. Citrate toxicity
Reveal answer

C. No liver, no citrate clearance: ionized calcium falls, and you get myocardial depression, vasodilation and a long QT. The anhepatic phase is the extreme version of a clamped Pringle or a small remnant. Treat with CaCl2 and follow iCa. Talk link: The one idea, citrate row.

Q5 · Advanced · ANESHUB general

During liver transplantation, sodium bicarbonate and calcium chloride are given just before reperfusion of the graft to counteract:

  1. coagulopathy
  2. decreased cardiac output
  3. hyperkalemia
  4. hypermagnesemia
  5. hypotension
Reveal answer

C. Reperfusion washes cold, acidic, potassium-rich effluent into the heart. Calcium stabilizes the membrane; bicarbonate shifts potassium into cells. Contrast with resection: after a Pringle the washout is mostly lactate, and routine bicarbonate isn't the answer. Talk link: Reperfusion.
