Stress dose steroids: where the 100 mg came from, and what the data actually say

A dogma built on two case reports in the 1950s, tested in the 1990s and 2010s, and slowly rewritten. Tabs follow the order you'd teach it at the head of the bed.

Two deaths, one dose, sixty years of habit

"Every stress dose order you've ever seen traces back to two case reports. The dose was a guess. The guess was reasonable. Then nobody tested it for thirty years."

1949
Cortisone arrivesHench and Kendall treat rheumatoid arthritis with cortisone (Nobel 1950). Suddenly lots of people are on chronic supraphysiologic glucocorticoids, and nobody yet knows what that does to their adrenals.
1952
Fraser, Preuss & Bigford (JAMA)A 34-year-old man with RA on cortisone has it stopped 2 days before hip surgery. He becomes hypotensive intraop, never recovers, and dies hours later. Autopsy: bilaterally atrophic adrenals. First described perioperative adrenal crisis from exogenous steroids.
1953
Lewis et al. (Ann Intern Med)A second, similar death: a patient on continuous cortisone, collapsed after surgery, adrenal atrophy at autopsy. Two cases are enough to change practice worldwide.
1950s–80s
The empiric eraPractice settles on roughly 200–300 mg/day of hydrocortisone, typically 100 mg IV q8h, for anyone who has "been on steroids." The number is chosen to match the maximum cortisol a healthy adrenal can make under extreme stress. It's a ceiling, given as a default.
1986
Udelsman et al. (J Clin Invest), the primate studyAdrenalectomized monkeys get 1/10×, 1×, or 10× normal cortisol replacement, then cholecystectomy. The 1/10× group is hypotensive with low SVR and contractility, and dies more often. Physiologic replacement was indistinguishable from supraphysiologic. Cortisol is necessary, but more than normal doesn't help.
1994
Salem et al. (Ann Surg)Review proposes matching the dose to the size of the operation: ~25 mg minor, 50–75 mg moderate, 100–150 mg major, for 1–3 days. The tiered scheme most of us were taught.
1997
Glowniak & Loriaux (Surgery)Small double-blind RCT in patients on chronic steroids who took their usual dose: saline vs stress dose. No difference in hemodynamics.
2008
Marik & Varon (Arch Surg)Systematic review: patients who keep taking their usual daily dose don't appear to need supplemental stress dosing for surgery. Patients with primary HPA disease still do.
2014
Zaghiyan et al. (Ann Surg), RCT, n=92Steroid-treated IBD patients having major colorectal surgery. HC 100 mg q8h vs IV equivalent of their usual dose. No postural hypotension on POD1 in 95% vs 96%: low dose was non-inferior.
2017
Liu, Reidy, Saatee & Collard (Anesthesiology)Risk-stratified approach: who is suppressed, who isn't, and graded supplementation for those in the middle. Most US anesthesia references now follow this.
2020
AAGBI / RCP / Society for Endocrinology (UK)Keeps stress dosing for anyone with AI, including ≥5 mg prednisolone for ≥4 weeks: HC 100 mg at induction, then 200 mg/24 h for major surgery.
2022
ACR / AAHKS (arthroplasty)For RA, SpA, SLE patients having hip or knee replacement: give the current daily dose, not a supraphysiologic stress dose.
2024
ESE / Endocrine Society joint guideline on glucocorticoid-induced AIAnything above ~15–25 mg hydrocortisone/day equivalent (prednisone 4–6 mg) can suppress the axis. Untested current or recent users should get stress coverage when stressed.
The tension to name out loudEndocrine and anesthesia guidelines still say "cover them." Rheumatology and the surgical RCTs say "usual dose is enough." Both are defensible. The difference is mostly about how much harm one hydrocortisone dose does (very little) versus how bad a missed adrenal crisis is (lethal).

What cortisol does, and how much a stressed body makes

"Cortisol isn't a pressor. It's permissive. It's the reason your phenylephrine works."

What cortisol does for blood pressure

  • Keeps α₁ and β receptors expressed and coupled, so catecholamines work.
  • Supports catecholamine synthesis (adrenal medulla PNMT needs high local cortisol).
  • Suppresses inducible NO synthase and vasodilatory prostaglandins.
  • Maintains cardiac contractility and capillary integrity.

Take it away and you get the Udelsman monkeys: low SVR, low contractility, and pressors that don't seem to do anything. That's adrenal crisis.

PearlHydrocortisone ≥50 mg/day saturates renal 11β-HSD2, so it acts as a full mineralocorticoid. That's why primary AI patients on stress-dose HC don't need fludrocortisone until the dose comes back down. The 8 mg of PONV dexamethasone we give at induction has zero mineralocorticoid effect but is roughly 200 mg of hydrocortisone in glucocorticoid terms, with a 36–72 h biologic half-life. Many 'unsupplemented' patients in practice were quietly stress dosed by the PONV prophylaxis.

Common ground in every scheme: never skip the usual daily dose. The single most common cause of perioperative adrenal crisis is the home steroid that got held for NPO and never restarted.

Refractory hypotension: floor vs ICU

"On the floor, steroids are for the patient whose adrenals can't answer. In the ICU, they're for the patient whose body won't listen to the answer."

Floor / PACU

Replacement for a broken axis

Classic page: POD1, home prednisone 10 mg held because NPO, now 78/40 after 2 L, confused, Na 129.

  1. Rule out the common things first and simultaneously: bleeding, sepsis, ischemia, PE, epidural, opioid excess.
  2. If there's any steroid exposure: draw a random cortisol (and ACTH if possible), then don't wait for it. HC 100 mg IV/IM now.
  3. Then 50 mg q6h or 200 mg/24 h infusion, plus isotonic fluid with dextrose.
  4. Response usually within hours. Taper back to maintenance over 1–3 days once the trigger resolves.

Any random cortisol <~15–18 µg/dL in a shocked patient is inappropriately low.

If you want to do a cosyntropin test later, dexamethasone 4 mg doesn't cross-react with the cortisol assay, but it has no mineralocorticoid effect. In known primary AI, use hydrocortisone.

Surviving Sepsis 2021: suggests IV hydrocortisone 200 mg/day for adults with septic shock and an ongoing vasopressor requirement (commonly NE ≥0.25 mcg/kg/min for ≥4 h). Weak recommendation.

CIRCI (2017 SCCM/ESICM): random total cortisol <10 µg/dL or rise <9 µg/dL after 250 mcg cosyntropin. Total cortisol is unreliable when albumin is low, so most intensivists treat the clinical picture.

Non-septic vasoplegia: weak data. Prophylactic steroids in cardiac surgery (DECS, SIRS trials) didn't improve outcomes.

Intraop versionChronic steroid user, unexplained hypotension that isn't answering phenylephrine or vasopressin. Run the usual list (depth, volume, bleeding, anaphylaxis, tension, tamponade, auto-PEEP). Then HC 100 mg IV is a cheap, low-harm move. It's rarely the answer, but it's the one you'll regret not trying.

The perioperative period and trauma

Elective surgery: what the RCTs did and didn't show

  • Every positive-sounding RCT (Glowniak 1997, Zaghiyan 2014, and similar small trials) enrolled patients who kept taking their usual dose. They show that continuing baseline is enough for most chronically treated patients. They don't show that zero steroid is safe.
  • The trials are small (dozens to ~100 patients) and adrenal crisis is rare, so none is powered to rule out a rare catastrophe. That's why guidelines still diverge.
  • Harms of a short stress course are modest: hyperglycemia, possibly wound and infection risk with longer courses, delirium in the elderly. Infection risk is the main reason ACR/AAHKS moved away from stress dosing in arthroplasty.

"The question isn't 'stress dose or nothing.' It's 'usual dose, or usual dose plus a little.' Everybody agrees on the usual dose."

Trauma

Trauma brings three separate steroid questions. Keep them apart, because the evidence points in different directions.

Don't

High-dose steroids for neuroprotection

CRASH (Lancet 2004), ~10,000 patients with TBI: 48 h of high-dose methylprednisolone increased 2-week mortality (21.1% vs 17.9%). Steroids for TBI are contraindicated.

Spinal cord injury (NASCIS II/III methylprednisolone): benefits were in post-hoc subgroups; infections and GI bleeding were real. AANS/CNS 2013 recommends against routine use. Some 2017 AO Spine guidance left a 24 h option within 8 h of injury. Follow your trauma/neurosurgery team.

Maybe

Low-dose HC for critical illness after trauma

HYPOLYTE (JAMA 2011), n=149: ventilated polytrauma patients. HC 200 mg/day × 5 d then taper reduced hospital-acquired pneumonia (35.6% vs 51.3%), added ~4 ventilator-free days, and reduced hyponatremia. No mortality difference.

Corti-TC (Lancet Respir Med 2014), n=336: same idea plus fludro in severe TBI. HAP 45% vs 53%, not significant (HR 0.75). Underpowered.

Not standard of care. Consider in vasopressor-dependent shock that persists after hemorrhage control and adequate resuscitation.

Do

The trauma patient on chronic steroids

Unplanned surgery, often missed home doses, and maximal stress. Treat as major surgery: HC 100 mg IV early (at induction is fine), then 50 mg q6–8h or 200 mg/24 h.

Clues they're on steroids when nobody can tell you: medical alert bracelet, cushingoid habitus, transplant scar, COPD/asthma, inflammatory bowel or rheumatologic disease, outside med list.

Know it

Etomidate in trauma

Etomidate blocks 11β-hydroxylase. A single induction dose suppresses cortisol synthesis for roughly 24 h (reported up to 72 h).

RSI trial (NEJM 2025), n=2,365 critically ill adults: ketamine vs etomidate, 28-day in-hospital mortality 28.1% vs 29.1% (NS). More peri-intubation cardiovascular collapse with ketamine (22.1% vs 17.0%).

Practical read: one dose of etomidate is a defensible choice for the unstable patient. If that patient later has vasopressor-dependent shock, remember the adrenal is temporarily offline and lower your threshold for hydrocortisone.

Is the axis suppressed? A risk ladder

Easy-to-miss exposures

  • Intra-articular or epidural triamcinolone in the last ~3 months.
  • High-dose inhaled fluticasone, especially with ritonavir/cobicistat or azole antifungals (CYP3A4 inhibition turns an inhaler into systemic steroid).
  • Megestrol (has glucocorticoid activity), opioids at high chronic doses, checkpoint-inhibitor hypophysitis.
  • Recent pituitary surgery, or a post-op adrenalectomy for Cushing's (the contralateral gland is suppressed).

Testing, if there's time

ESE/ES 2024 uses 8 AM cortisol, drawn ~24 h after the last dose: >10 µg/dL (300 nmol/L) means the axis has likely recovered; 5–10 is indeterminate; <5 µg/dL (150 nmol/L) means likely AI. These cutoffs only apply to unstressed outpatients. Don't use them on a sick inpatient.

Bottom line for the ORTrue AI (primary or secondary): stress dose every time, give the usual dose too, and don't stop the fludro in primary AI unless HC is ≥50 mg/day. Chronic steroid users: never miss the usual dose; add supplementation by risk tier and size of surgery; treat unexplained shock with HC early.

Drawing it in 5 minutes

  1. The axis. Three stacked boxes: hypothalamus, pituitary, adrenal. Arrow down, dotted feedback arrow back up. Add "pred" off to the side pressing on the top two boxes. Say: "the adrenal isn't broken, it's unemployed."
  2. The curve. Flat baseline, bump at incision that peaks around emergence, back down by day 2–3. Write 10 mg/day at baseline and ~100–150 at the peak. Ask: "So why did we give 300?"
  3. The monkeys. Three bars: 1/10×, 1×, 10× replacement. X out the first. Draw an equals sign between the other two. That's Udelsman, and that's the whole talk.
  4. The fork. One box "refractory hypotension," two arrows: floor (replace a missing hormone) and ICU (modulate shock). Write the ICU dose under it: 200 mg/day.
  5. The ladder. A line marked <5, 5–20, >20 mg pred. Green, yellow, red.

Questions that make it stick

  • Your patient on prednisone 20 mg got 8 mg dexamethasone for PONV at induction. How much hydrocortisone is that? Do they still need a stress dose today?
  • Why can a patient with secondary AI tolerate a missed dose better than one with Addison's? (Think aldosterone.)
  • ADRENAL showed no mortality benefit. Why do we still give hydrocortisone in septic shock?
  • You induced a trauma patient with etomidate. Twelve hours later they're on norepinephrine 0.3 after the bleeding is controlled. What's your next move?

Board-style questions

Original questions written for this talk in ABA style. Swap in qvault questions on the Notion page if you want the bank's versions.

1. A 58-year-old with RA has taken prednisone 7.5 mg daily for 4 years and is scheduled for elective total knee arthroplasty. Per the 2022 ACR/AAHKS guideline, the most appropriate perioperative glucocorticoid plan is:
A. Hold prednisone the morning of surgery
B. Continue the usual daily dose without supplementation
C. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV q8h for 72 h
D. Dexamethasone 10 mg IV at induction in place of the prednisone
E. Obtain a cosyntropin stimulation test before proceeding
B. ACR/AAHKS recommends continuing the current daily dose rather than supraphysiologic stress dosing for RA, SpA and SLE patients having THA/TKA. Holding the usual dose (A) is the classic cause of crisis. Endocrine and UK guidelines would add hydrocortisone; know that the specialties differ.
2. Which finding best distinguishes primary adrenal insufficiency from secondary adrenal insufficiency?
A. Hypotension
B. Hyponatremia
C. Hyperkalemia
D. Hypoglycemia
E. Eosinophilia
C. Primary AI destroys the zona glomerulosa, so aldosterone is lost and potassium rises. In secondary/tertiary AI the renin-angiotensin system keeps aldosterone going. Hyponatremia occurs in both (in secondary AI via cortisol deficiency and ADH release).
3. In adrenalectomized primates undergoing cholecystectomy (Udelsman 1986), compared with physiologic cortisol replacement, supraphysiologic (10×) replacement resulted in:
A. Lower mortality
B. Higher systemic vascular resistance
C. Impaired wound healing
D. No measurable hemodynamic or survival difference
E. More postoperative hyperglycemia and sepsis
D. Physiologic and supraphysiologic groups were indistinguishable from sham controls. Only the subphysiologic (1/10×) group had hypotension, low SVR, low contractility and higher mortality.
4. Etomidate causes adrenal suppression by inhibiting which enzyme?
A. 21-hydroxylase
B. 11β-hydroxylase
C. 17α-hydroxylase
D. Aldosterone synthase only
E. 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2
B. 11β-hydroxylase converts 11-deoxycortisol to cortisol. Inhibition after a single dose lasts roughly 24 h. (11β-HSD2, choice E, is the renal enzyme that keeps cortisol off the mineralocorticoid receptor; high-dose hydrocortisone saturates it.)
5. A patient with Addison's disease on hydrocortisone 15 mg AM / 5 mg PM and fludrocortisone 0.1 mg daily is having a Whipple. Which plan is most appropriate?
A. Usual oral doses only
B. Hydrocortisone 25 mg IV at induction only
C. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction, then 200 mg/24 h; fludrocortisone may be held while HC ≥50 mg/day
D. Dexamethasone 4 mg IV q6h alone
E. Double the fludrocortisone and omit hydrocortisone
C. True AI always gets stress dosing for major surgery. At ≥50 mg/day, hydrocortisone provides full mineralocorticoid activity. Dexamethasone (D) has no mineralocorticoid effect, which matters most in primary AI.
6. Which septic shock trial showed a reduction in 90-day mortality with hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone?
A. CORTICUS
B. ADRENAL
C. APROCCHSS
D. HYPOLYTE
E. CRASH
C. APROCCHSS (2018): 43.0% vs 49.1%. ADRENAL (hydrocortisone alone, infusion) and CORTICUS showed faster shock reversal without mortality benefit. HYPOLYTE was trauma; CRASH was high-dose methylprednisolone in TBI and increased mortality.
