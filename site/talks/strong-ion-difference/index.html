Bicarbonate is a result, not a cause. The acid-base variable you control most in the OR is the sodium-to-chloride gap in whatever you're hanging.

Three independent variables

Stewart's approach says pH and bicarbonate are dependent: they move only when one of three independent variables moves.

Draw this: the three dials

VariableWhat sets itDirection

AtotWeak acids: albumin, phosphateLow albumin is alkalinizing and hides acidosis
SIDStrong cations minus strong anionsLow SID = acidosis; high SID = alkalosis

SID = (Na⁺ + K⁺ + Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺) − (Cl⁻ + lactate⁻ + other strong anions). At the bedside, when K, Ca and Mg are being replaced and ketones aren't the issue, Na − Cl is close enough. Normal is about +38 to +40.

Every liter you hang has its own SID. Anything with a SID below plasma bicarbonate (about 24) pushes the patient toward acidosis; anything above pushes toward alkalosis. Saline's SID is zero.

Why low albumin matters

Albumin is a weak acid. Losing it is alkalinizing, so a hypoalbuminemic patient's pH looks better than their acid load. Rough size: base excess shifts by about 0.25 × (42 − albumin in g/L) mEq/L. Albumin 1.8 g/dL (18 g/L) hides about 6 mEq/L of acidosis. For the anion gap, add 2.5 for every 1 g/dL albumin is below 4.

ASK THE LEARNERpH 7.30 after a 6-hour ex-lap, bicarb 18, lactate 1.5. Do you give bicarb? (No. Look at Na − Cl first. If it's 28, you gave them this acidosis with saline, and bicarb treats the dependent variable.)

Trauma and massive transfusion

The hemorrhaging patient takes several SID hits at the same time.

  1. Lactate (strong anion up). Hypoperfusion. Every mmol/L of lactate narrows SID by about 1. Fix: perfusion and source control.
  2. Chloride (iatrogenic). Saline is 154:154; plasma is about 140:100. Two to four liters in the field and chloride sits at 112–116, SID in the low 20s to 30. Fix: stop the saline.
  3. Calcium (strong cation down). Citrate in every unit binds ionized calcium, and a shocked, cold liver can't clear it. Low iCa lowers SID and also depresses the heart and clotting. Fix: CaCl2 1 g every ~4 units or when iCa < 1.1.
  4. Albumin (Atot down, masking). Dilution and loss lower albumin, which hides part of the acidosis. pH 7.25 with albumin 1.8 is worse than it looks.

The lethal triad is a Stewart spiral

Cold slows citrate clearance, which drops calcium and SID. Acidosis impairs clotting factors, so more bleeding, more lactate, more product, more citrate.

Action map

  • CaCl2 early and often; check iCa on every gas.
  • Balanced resuscitation with blood products; no saline boluses.
  • Warm actively: it restores citrate metabolism.
  • Avoid bicarbonate drips: they treat the dependent variable.
  • Permissive hypotension until the source is controlled.

No single insult; just eight hours of small ones.

Strategy

  • Balanced crystalloid as maintenance, goal-directed boluses.
  • Serial gases every ~2 hours; write Na − Cl next to every one.
  • 5% albumin for volume in protein-depleted patients restores Atot.
  • Expect a transient lactate bump after unclamping; don't reflexively bolus it.

Liver resection deserves its own talk: the organ being resected is the one that clears lactate and citrate. See SID in Hepatic Resection.

The ICU handoff

Name each component of the acidosis separately, so the ICU doesn't reach for bicarbonate or a sepsis workup.

"His pH is 7.28, base excess −10. Na minus Cl is about 25: he started hyperchloremic from chronic biliary drainage and got 7 liters of PlasmaLyte. His albumin is 1.9, which is alkalinizing him, so the true acid load is worse than the pH suggests. Lactate is 2.4 and falling since reperfusion. Calcium was 0.94 after the anastomosis; he got 2 g of CaCl2 and the last iCa was 1.12. This is a mixed acidosis: a chloride component plus residual lactate. He doesn't need bicarb. He needs perfusion pressure, calcium every 4 hours, and no saline."

Board questions

Q1 · Clinical · PassMachine · renal & electrolytes

A 50-year-old woman has a right hemicolectomy. Postop she is oliguric, presumed hypovolemic, and is getting IV crystalloid. Which statement best describes 0.9% NaCl?

  1. It is a balanced crystalloid
  2. Isotonic saline infusion may cause hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis
  3. Excess water is excreted faster with 0.9% NaCl than with LR
Reveal answer

B. Saline's SID is zero, so every liter drags plasma SID toward zero. That's the chart at the top. C is backwards: saline loads are excreted more slowly. Talk link: the hero simulator and Fluids.

Q2 · Basic · ANESHUB pharm

Preop labs: Na 140, Cl 90, PaCO2 46, pH 7.50. These findings most suggest prior treatment with:

  1. acetazolamide
  2. enalapril
  3. furosemide
  4. mannitol
  5. spironolactone
Reveal answer

C. Na − Cl = 50: a high SID, so alkalosis, with a little respiratory compensation. Loop diuretics waste more chloride than sodium. Acetazolamide does the opposite (bicarbonate wasting, hyperchloremic acidosis). Talk link: The one idea, "high SID means alkalosis."

Q3 · Advanced · ANESHUB respiratory & critical care

A 20-kg 5-year-old with a cerebral contusion, on day 5, NG tube draining to gravity, dexamethasone, D5 ½NS and D5LR. ABG: PaO2 120, PaCO2 44, pH 7.48, BE +6. Na 140, K 3.2, Cl 91. Urine output 15 mL/h. This patient most likely requires:

  1. additional IV fluids
  2. furosemide diuresis
  3. ammonium chloride
  4. reinstitution of mechanical ventilation
  5. potassium chloride
Reveal answer

E. The NG tube is draining HCl. Chloride loss raises SID (140 − 91 = 49), producing a chloride-responsive alkalosis, and the kidney trades potassium to hold sodium. The fix is to give back the strong anion: KCl replaces both. Ammonium chloride also supplies chloride, but it's an outdated and riskier way to do it. Talk link: The one idea.

Q4 · Basic · ANESHUB respiratory & critical care

During cardiac arrest with effective compressions and positive-pressure ventilation, 50 mEq of sodium bicarbonate is given. The most likely result is:

  1. decreased mixed venous pH
  2. decreased SpO2
  3. increased PetCO2
  4. increased plasma lactate
  5. increased serum potassium
Reveal answer

C. Bicarbonate buffers acid into CO2 and water, and that CO2 shows up in exhaled gas. Stewart framing: bicarb does raise SID (it adds sodium without chloride), but if ventilation can't clear the CO2, you've traded a metabolic acidosis for a respiratory one, including inside cells. Talk link: Trauma & MTP, "avoid bicarbonate drips."

Q5 · Advanced · ANESHUB general

A 20-year-old with poorly controlled diabetes needs urgent drainage of an abdominal abscess. After 10 units of regular insulin and 5 units/h for 5 hours: Na 128, K 5.4, Cl 80, glucose 750. The most appropriate perioperative management is:

  1. add subcutaneous NPH insulin
  2. sodium bicarbonate to correct metabolic acidosis
  3. normal saline to correct the presumed hypovolemia
  4. furosemide to correct hyponatremia
  5. D5 0.2% saline to prevent hypoglycemia
Reveal answer

C. The deficit is volume, and the sodium is low partly from glucose pulling water out of cells. Stewart twist: Na − Cl is 48, which looks alkalotic, but ketoacids are unmeasured strong anions that close the gap, which is why bicarbonate isn't the answer. Newer DKA data increasingly favor balanced crystalloid over saline, but of these options, volume is the right call. Talk link: The one idea, "other strong anions."

Framework: Stewart PA, Can J Physiol Pharmacol 1983;61:1444–61; albumin correction after Figge and Fencl. Fluid compositions are typical US values; check your institution's products, especially albumin.
