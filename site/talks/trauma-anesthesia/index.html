Trauma Anesthesia: A Phase-Based Approach

OR chalk talk · MS3 – CA-2 · DRAFT v0.3

The big picture

A trauma case is a resuscitation with an anesthetic layered on top of it. Hemorrhage is the leading cause of preventable trauma death, so every phase comes back to the same question: is this patient bleeding, and are we winning?

Three ideas to leave with
  • Prepare with questions, not a checklist you memorize. Six quick questions set up the room, the team and your goals.
  • Resuscitate, then induce. ATLS 11 now puts exsanguination first (xABCDE) and stresses treating shock before RSI.
  • Get data early, then let it steer you. Start with ratios, switch to TEG and labs as soon as you have them.
The lethal diamond

The old "triad" plus calcium. Citrate in every unit of blood binds calcium, and low iCa worsens both clotting and contractility.

Phase 1 · Before the OR

You often get 5–15 minutes of warning. Run through these questions out loud with your team. The answers set your room, your people and your initial goals.

1. What's the situation?Who is coming, how sick, and what has been done already? Is the airway secured? Is MTP activated? How much blood have they had, including any low-titer O whole blood (LTOWB) in the ED? Was TXA given, and when? Is a tourniquet or binder on? Prehospital vitals and the trend matter more than a single set.
2. What equipment do we need in the room?Rapid infuser primed, blood warmer, MTP coolers, airway setup, line kits, TEG sample tubes. Full list below.
3. Who do we need, and do we need extra hands?A second anesthesia provider or attending, an anesthesia tech, someone dedicated to the rapid infuser, a blood runner, and someone keeping the blood log. Call early. Sending help away is easier than finding it mid-crisis.
4. When are we going to the OR?Straight from the ED, or CT first? An unstable patient does not go to the scanner. "Stable enough for CT" is a team decision with the trauma surgeon, and it should be re-asked if the vitals change.
5. Which OR or site?Main trauma OR, hybrid OR or IR (embolization or REBOA), or the ED (resuscitative thoracotomy)? Every site away from home base means bringing your drugs, airway gear, infuser and monitors with you.
6. What's the mechanism, and how likely is bleeding?Penetrating torso injury, high-energy blunt trauma, or a pelvic fracture means assume hemorrhage until proven otherwise. Estimate it with the tools below.

Estimating bleeding risk

Shock index = HR ÷ SBP
  • < 0.7: reassuring
  • > 1.0: think significant hemorrhage
  • Trend it. A rising SI is worse than any single number.

Caveats: beta-blockers, pacemakers, elderly patients, and young patients who compensate until they suddenly don't.

ABC score (1 point each)
  • Penetrating mechanism
  • SBP ≤ 90 on arrival
  • HR ≥ 120 on arrival
  • Positive FAST

≥ 2 points predicts massive transfusion. Have blood in the room.

Room setup

Resuscitation
  • Rapid infuser primed, plus fluid warmer
  • MTP activated; first component cooler (RBC : plasma : platelets) in the room or on the way
  • Count LTOWB units given in the ED. They go in the blood log.
  • TXA available, but held until the TEG shows lysis
  • Calcium chloride 1 g × several
  • Forced-air warmer; room temperature turned up
  • Cell saver, if no bowel contamination is expected
Airway
  • Video laryngoscope first, DL backup
  • Bougie, two working suctions (big bore)
  • SGA rescue
  • Cric kit open in the room
  • Someone assigned to MILS
Drugs
  • Ketamine or etomidate (reduced dose)
  • Rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg (± sux)
  • Push-dose phenylephrine and epinephrine
  • Norepinephrine / vasopressin infusions ready
  • Small-dose fentanyl; midazolam / scopolamine for amnesia
Access & monitoring
  • Large-bore PIV / RIC kits
  • Introducer (8.5–9 Fr) kit + ultrasound
  • Arterial line kit + transducers zeroed
  • IO drill (humeral)
  • Pre-labeled lab tubes: ABG, CBC, BMP/iCa, coags, fibrinogen, TEG, type & cross

From questions → initial goals

If the answers say…Then the initial goal is…
Unstable, penetrating, ABC ≥ 2Get to surgical control fast. Prep and drape before induction. Blood first, induce last. Accept permissive hypotension.
Blunt multi-system with TBIProtect the brain: no hypotension, no hypoxia. Target SBP ≥ 110 / MAP ≥ 80. Normocapnia.
Stable, single injury, CT doneA more controlled induction. You have time for lines first. Still check a baseline TEG.

Case start huddle (30 seconds): "This is our suspected injury. This is our BP goal. Blood is here or coming. I'll induce when you're prepped. Here's what happens if they arrest." Say it before the patient rolls in.

Phase 2 · Arrival & Induction: xABCDE

Use the same primary survey language as the trauma team (ATLS 11), viewed through an anesthesia lens. You assess and treat each letter as you find it; they happen in parallel, not in strict order.

x

eXsanguinating hemorrhage

  • Tourniquets, packing and pelvic binders stay on. Don't let them come off during the transfer to the OR table.
  • Ask: "Where is the bleeding, and when can you control it?"
  • In the crashing patient, prep and drape before induction. Induction removes sympathetic tone, and PPV and relaxation can release tamponade.
  • Get transfusion running through the best access you have before you push drugs.
A

Airway + C-spine

  • Assume a full stomach and an unstable C-spine. Open the collar's front and use MILS.
  • Video laryngoscope first-line (ATLS 11). Bougie ready.
  • Arrives already intubated? Confirm ETCO₂, tube depth, and that it isn't a right mainstem. Don't trust the handoff.
  • Airway distorted by neck or face trauma: double setup with the surgeon scrubbed for a surgical airway.
B

Breathing

  • Before PPV, think: is there a pneumothorax? Positive pressure can turn a simple PTX into a tension PTX.
  • Sudden hypotension, high peak pressures and unilateral breath sounds after intubation → tension PTX until proven otherwise. Decompress.
  • PPV lowers preload. Start with modest PEEP and lung-protective volumes.
  • Normocapnia (ETCO₂ often underestimates PaCO₂ in shock). Hyperventilate only as a bridge for active herniation.
C

Circulation: resuscitate before you induce

  • ATLS 11 stresses treating shock before RSI. Even 1–2 units before induction can prevent an induction arrest.
  • Permissive hypotension until bleeding is controlled: SBP 80–90 / MAP 50–65 (no TBI).
  • TBI changes the target: SBP ≥ 110 / MAP ≥ 80. One hypotensive episode worsens outcome.
  • Blood, not crystalloid. The ED may have bridged with whole blood. In the OR we continue with empiric 1:1:1 components through the rapid infuser until the first TEG is back.
D

Disability

  • Document GCS and pupils before induction. After that, the neuro exam is gone for hours.
  • Lateralizing signs or a blown pupil → discuss with neurosurgery and the team before induction. It may change the OR plan (e.g., craniotomy first vs. laparotomy).
E

Exposure / Environment

  • Fully expose to find injuries, then warm aggressively: room temperature up, forced air, warmed fluids, cover what isn't prepped.
  • Each 1 °C drop measurably impairs coagulation, and it doesn't show on standard labs run at 37 °C.

Awareness: Low-dose anesthetic in a scared, hypotensive patient is a setup for recall. Give amnestics (midazolam or scopolamine) once hemodynamics allow, and titrate volatile up as you resuscitate.

Phase 3 · Access: Selection & Site

Flow is about the catheter, not about "central vs. peripheral." Short and fat beats long and thin.

Poiseuille in one line: Flow ∝ r⁴ ÷ length

Classic resident trap: a 16 G triple-lumen port is long and narrow and flows worse than a 16 G peripheral IV. It's for pressors, not volume.

Access hierarchy

StepWhatNotes
1Two large-bore PIVs (14–16 G)Often what you arrive with. Check that they flush and don't infiltrate under pressure.
2RIC / large-bore peripheral exchangeExchange over a wire from an existing PIV. Introducer-level flow without a central stick.
3IO (humeral preferred)Bridge access in seconds. Humeral flows better than tibial. Painful under pressure in awake patients.
4Introducer (MAC / Cordis) in IJ, subclavian or femoralUse the side port for volume. A pressor lumen can be added through the introducer.
5Arterial lineEarly, for beat-to-beat BP and labs, but don't delay incision for it. The surgeon can place a femoral A-line from the field.

Site selection: put volume where it will reach the heart

Abdominal / pelvic bleeding

Put access above the diaphragm (arms, IJ, subclavian). Volume given through a femoral line may pour out an injured IVC or iliac vein.

Chest injury

If you go subclavian, go on the side of the chest injury or existing chest tube so you don't risk a PTX on the "good" lung. If a great-vessel or subclavian injury is suspected, infuse from the other side or below the diaphragm.

Extremity injury

Don't place access distal to a proximal vascular injury or tourniquet on the same limb.

C-spine precautions

IJ is harder with a collar and MILS. Subclavian or femoral may be faster, and ultrasound still helps.

First stick = first labs. Every new line is a chance to draw the baseline panel (next tab). Have pre-labeled tubes in hand before you place it.

Phase 4 · Post-Induction & Initial Goals

The tube is in and the surgeon is cutting. Now establish time zero: a full lab and TEG panel so every later decision has something to compare against.

The time-zero bundle (send within minutes of induction)

Point-of-care / ABG
  • pH, PaCO₂, base deficit
  • Lactate
  • Hgb
  • Ionized Ca²⁺, K⁺, glucose
Viscoelastic
  • TEG 6s, citrated trauma (global hemostasis) cartridge: CK-R, CRT-MA, CFF-MA, CK-LY30
  • Early amplitudes in ~10 min; LY30 needs ~30 min after MA
  • Shows which part of clotting is failing
Send-out labs
  • CBC (platelets)
  • PT/INR, PTT
  • Fibrinogen
  • Type & cross (if not done)
Why both TEG and conventional labs? Standard coags take 30–60 min, are run on plasma at 37 °C, and don't show fibrinolysis or platelet function. TEG is whole blood, fast, and functional. Conventional labs are still useful for confirmation and trending.

TXA: only for lysis

  • Our practice: give TXA only when the TEG shows lysis: LY30 ≥ 3% (hyperfibrinolysis).
  • Why not everyone? Many trauma patients are in fibrinolysis shutdown, and blanket TXA may add risk without benefit.
  • When indicated: 1 g IV over 10 min, then 1 g over 8 h, ideally within 3 h of injury. In CRASH-2, giving it after 3 h was associated with more bleeding deaths.
  • Always ask whether EMS or the ED already gave it, so the patient isn't dosed twice.
Say it out loud at ~15 minutes: "Time-zero labs sent at __. First TEG back at __. ED gave __ LTOWB. We've given __ RBC / __ plasma / __ platelets, Ca __ g. TXA given / held (LY30 __)." This keeps the room on the same page and starts your blood log.

Phase 5 · Mid-Case & Resuscitation

Shift from empiric ratios (you're blind) to goal-directed, TEG-based replacement (you can see). Keep repeating the cycle: give, check, adjust.

Published thresholds, compiled for teaching. Einersen's rapid-TEG cutoffs were derived on the TEG 5000; the 6s CRT channel correlates closely with it (Neal 2020). Bedside judgment and the clinical picture still win.

The key 6s trick: subtract

CRT-MA − CFF-MA ≈ the platelet contribution (iTACTIC treated platelets when this was < 45 mm).

  • CRT-MA low, CFF-MA low (< 20) → fibrinogen first (cryo), then recheck
  • CRT-MA low, CFF-MA normal → platelets
  • Both normal but still bleeding → surgical bleeding, or check iCa, temp, pH
The memory hook

R = "Ready? Factors start it." CFF = "Fibrinogen Foundation." CRT-MA = "Muscle: platelets make it strong." LY = "Lysis: the only time we reach for TXA."

Don't wait for MA

On the 6s, early amplitudes predict a low final MA (Vigstedt 2022, iTACTIC data):

  • CRT A10 < 45 mm → MA will be < 55. Get platelets moving.
  • CFF A10 < 15 mm → FF-MA will be low. Get cryo moving.

CRT takes ~21 min to reach MA; CFF takes ~12 min. A10 lets you act ~10 minutes sooner.

Fibrinolysis phenotypes (why TXA is lysis-guided)
  • Hyperfibrinolysis (LY30 ≥ 3%): 18% of patients, 44% mortality, mostly from bleeding. TXA helps.
  • Physiologic (0.81–2.9%): 18% of patients, 3% mortality
  • Shutdown (≤ 0.8%): the most common phenotype (64%), 17% mortality, mostly from organ failure. Extra TXA adds risk without benefit.

Moore HB et al. 2014, Denver Health: a U-shaped mortality curve. This is the rationale for lysis-guided TXA.

Order of operations when several values are off: fix the "systemic" problems first (iCa > 1.1, temp > 36, pH > 7.2, Hgb ~7–9) because they make every number look worse. Then treat lysis → fibrinogen → factors → platelets, and recheck the TEG after each round.

The resus loop: every ~30 min or every cooler

The anesthesia–surgery huddle (say it, don't assume it)
  • Totals: RBC / plasma / platelets / cryo / cell saver / crystalloid
  • Trend: are pressor needs going up or down? Lactate?
  • Temp, pH, iCa, latest TEG
  • "How close are you to control?"
When to push for damage control surgery (pack, temporary closure, get to the ICU):
  • pH < 7.2 or worsening base deficit
  • Temp < 35 °C
  • Coagulopathy on TEG despite targeted replacement
  • Ongoing massive transfusion with no surgical control
Anesthesia sees this first. Saying "I think we need to pack and go" is part of our job.

Other mid-case watch-outs

  • Hypocalcemia (citrate): the most commonly missed, and easy to fix
  • Hyperkalemia from stored RBCs, worse with acidosis and low flow. Peaked T waves mean act now.
  • Pressors: a bridge, not a replacement for volume. Vasopressin is reasonable in refractory hemorrhagic shock.
  • TACO / TRALI, abdominal compartment syndrome, and missed injuries (re-check the chest if ventilation changes)

Phase 6 · End of Case

Two very different endings. Both need a plan, and both deserve the same care.

A. Transfer to the ICU

Before you leave the OR
  • Final ABG / lactate / iCa / K and a last TEG (note the time)
  • Call the blood bank: deactivate MTP or keep it open, and return unused products
  • Most stay intubated (open abdomen, ongoing resus, TBI)
  • Pressors on pumps; enough blood for transport
  • Full monitors + ambu/vent + airway gear + push-dose pressor for transport
Warm handoff

Bedside, monitors on, ICU team listening, surgeon present if possible.

ICU HANDOFF (trauma) • Who / mechanism: __ y/o, __ mechanism, arrived __ • Injuries & procedures: __ (packed? open abdomen? return to OR planned at __) • Airway: grade __ view, tube __ at __ cm, C-spine status __ • Blood totals: RBC __ | plasma __ | platelets __ | cryo __ | WB __ | cell saver __ | crystalloid __ • Drugs: TXA (time __) | CaCl __ g | pressors now __ • Last labs (time __): pH __ BD __ lactate __ Hgb __ iCa __ K __ | TEG: R __ α __ MA __ LY30 __ • Temp __ °C • Lines: __ (site, size, what's running where) • Concerns / to-do: tertiary survey, imaging still needed, family updated?

B. Intraoperative death

Recognizing futility is a team decision, made with the surgeon
  • Ongoing exsanguination with no achievable surgical control
  • Prolonged arrest without return of circulation despite correcting reversible causes (Hs & Ts: hypovolemia, tension PTX, tamponade, hyperK, hypothermia)
  • Profound, unresponsive physiology (e.g., severe acidosis, refractory coagulopathy)
In the room
  • Explicit pause: "Is everyone in agreement to stop?"
  • Time of death announced and recorded
  • Leave all lines, tubes and drains in place (medical examiner case)
  • Notify the blood bank: stop MTP
  • Notify the organ procurement organization (required)
After
  • Accurate anesthesia record, including blood totals and events
  • Family conversation with the surgeon; chaplain / social work
  • Team debrief, even 2 minutes
  • Check on your residents, students and staff. Use peer support resources.

Board-style questions

Original practice questions in ABA style. Tap to reveal each answer.

Q1. A 28-year-old with a GSW to the abdomen arrives in the OR with HR 135 and BP 78/50. He is awake and anxious. The surgeons are prepping. Compared with a healthy patient, which change in induction is MOST appropriate?
  1. Increase the propofol dose because of high sympathetic tone
  2. Reduce the hypnotic dose and increase the rocuronium dose
  3. Avoid neuromuscular blockade to preserve spontaneous ventilation
  4. Delay induction until the arterial line is placed
Answer

B. In hemorrhagic shock, a smaller central volume and a larger share of cardiac output going to the brain mean a lower hypnotic dose works, and a full dose causes cardiovascular collapse. Low cardiac output slows the onset of the NMB, so 1.2 mg/kg rocuronium gives faster intubating conditions. Arterial line placement should not delay a life-saving laparotomy (D).

Q2. During a massive transfusion for a pelvic fracture, a TEG 6s shows: CK-R 8.2 min, CRT-MA 44 mm, CFF-MA 24 mm, CK-LY30 0.8%. iCa is 1.18 and temperature is 36.4 °C. Which product is MOST indicated?
  1. Plasma
  2. Cryoprecipitate
  3. Platelets
  4. Tranexamic acid
Answer

C. CRT-MA (total clot strength) is low but CFF-MA (fibrinogen only) is normal, so the deficit is platelets. CK-R is normal, so factors are adequate. LY30 is low, which suggests shutdown, so TXA is not indicated. The systemic factors (Ca, temp) are already corrected.

Q3. Forty minutes into a massive transfusion protocol, the patient becomes more hypotensive despite ongoing product replacement. The ECG shows a prolonged QT interval. The MOST likely cause is:
  1. Hyperkalemia from stored red cells
  2. Citrate-induced hypocalcemia
  3. Transfusion-related acute lung injury
  4. Hypomagnesemia from crystalloid dilution
Answer

B. Citrate in blood products chelates ionized calcium, especially with rapid transfusion and reduced hepatic clearance in shock. Hypocalcemia causes hypotension, reduced contractility, coagulopathy and QT prolongation. Treat with calcium chloride. Hyperkalemia (A) gives peaked T waves and a wide QRS, not a long QT.

Q4. Which change to an IV catheter will produce the LARGEST increase in gravity-driven flow?
  1. Halving its length
  2. Doubling its radius
  3. Doubling the height of the fluid bag
  4. Warming the fluid from 20 °C to 37 °C
Answer

B. By Hagen–Poiseuille, flow ∝ r⁴/(ηL). Doubling the radius increases flow 16-fold. Halving the length (A) and doubling the pressure gradient (C) each roughly double flow. Warming lowers viscosity modestly (D).

Q5. A trauma patient is now 4 hours from injury. A TEG 6s shows CK-LY30 of 0.3%. The surgical resident asks whether to give TXA "just in case." The BEST response is:
  1. Give 1 g TXA because it reduces mortality in all bleeding trauma patients
  2. Give 2 g TXA because the patient is beyond the 3-hour window
  3. Withhold TXA: there is no lysis, and late administration has been associated with increased bleeding death
  4. Give aminocaproic acid in place of TXA
Answer

C. An LY30 this low suggests fibrinolysis shutdown, so an antifibrinolytic adds risk without benefit. In CRASH-2, TXA given after 3 hours was associated with an increased risk of death from bleeding. Antifibrinolytics are indicated for documented lysis, ideally within 3 hours of injury.
